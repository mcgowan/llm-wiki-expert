# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dr. Sarah Wakeman, a board-certified addiction medicine physician, Harvard professor and fellowship program director at a large Boston academic medical center, whose mission is to change how people understand alcohol and drug problems and to bring addiction care back into mainstream medicine.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE] Wakeman — who lost an immediate family member to addiction while she was in medical school — argues that the core failure of how society treats addiction is framing it as a moral or willpower issue rather than a health condition: addiction is defined by "use despite consequences" and the four C's (loss of control, compulsive use, consequences, craving), with risk driven roughly 40-60% by genetics and much of the rest by trauma, especially adverse childhood experiences — "trauma is the gateway drug."[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]

The episode's centerpiece is a myth-busting walk through alcohol's health effects. Wakeman explains that the famous J-shaped curve suggesting moderate drinking is protective was an artifact of comparing drinkers to abstainers who were often sick or in recovery; when the reference group is corrected, the apparent benefits disappear, and she states plainly that she "would never say drinking alcohol is good for your health" — comparing it instead to dessert, bacon or sunbathing: a risk one may choose with eyes open.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE] She also makes the guest's broader case that connection is the opposite of addiction, that rehab as commonly practiced is a short-term fix lacking evidence-based care, and that effective treatment — medications, psychotherapy, empathy and hope — exists but rarely reaches people.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]

# Key points

- Scale of the problem as Wakeman presents it: 2.6 million alcohol-related deaths globally per year (about 7,000 per day) plus 600,000 drug-related deaths; roughly 400 million people worldwide have alcohol use disorder and 80 million a drug use disorder; lifetime prevalence of an alcohol problem may reach one in three people.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- The pandemic drove a 23% increase in US alcohol-related mortality from early 2020 and record overdose deaths, depressing US life expectancy until recently; frontline workers and caregivers showed the largest increases in use.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Alcohol acts as "an anti-anxiety and a pain medication all-in-one": it releases dopamine, binds the GABA system like benzodiazepines, and triggers endogenous opioid release — which is why naltrexone-style medication that blocks opioid receptors reduces drinking, and why the Sinclair method of taking it as-needed before drinking occasions leaves people satisfied after one drink.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- UK low-risk guidance is under 14 units a week (a unit being 8g of alcohol), but a typical large glass of wine is around three units — so a nightly glass already exceeds the low-risk threshold; even below those limits, breast and esophageal cancer risk rises (about a 5% relative increase in breast cancer, on a 13% baseline lifetime risk for US women), and moderate drinking is associated with essentially every form of cancer, with roughly 40% increased risk at two large glasses a day — a level many people consider normal.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- The mechanism is systemic, not organ-specific: ethanol's toxic breakdown byproduct (acetaldehyde) and the inflammation and reactive oxygen species generated in clearing it damage DNA; alcohol also multiplies tobacco's carcinogenic effects rather than merely adding to them.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- On the liver: it can regenerate even after losing 80% of its mass, but only up to the point of cirrhosis (her "eggs already baked into the muffin" analogy); binge surges are likely more damaging than steady moderate intake, and Wakeman now sees people in their twenties dying of fulminant alcoholic liver failure who never knew they were at risk — obesity and alcohol being the leading drivers of liver disease and transplant.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Heavy use visibly shrinks the brain: she shows an MRI of a 43-year-old with severe alcohol use disorder whose brain resembles a 90-year-old with dementia, and notes alcohol-related dementia and nutrition-related amnesia; heart effects include "holiday heart" atrial fibrillation and cardiomyopathy.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Alcohol distributes into body water, not fat — so people with more body fat get drunk faster, and "handling your drink" does not protect against liver, cancer or heart harms; hangovers track how high brain ethanol concentration gets, not just dehydration.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Wakeman is critical of the rehab industry: her team's secret-shopper study of US rehabs found many offering unproven treatments like horse or dolphin therapy while lacking the medications and evidence-based psychotherapies (CBT, motivational enhancement, trauma work) that actually help — and she rejects the "hit rock bottom" and tough-love models, noting a 130-fold increased drug-death risk after prison release, arguing people change through hope, not accumulated pain.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Emerging treatments she highlights: a landmark psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy trial that substantially reduced drinking, and GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (Wegovy/Ozempic class) which placebo-controlled trials now show reduce alcohol use, apparently resetting craving globally.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- For families she teaches CRAFT (community reinforcement and family training) and contingency management over confrontational interventions, demonstrates motivational interviewing live with Bartlett (amplifying "change talk," avoiding the "righting reflex"), and distinguishes protecting yourself from the myth that kicking someone out makes them better; therapist empathy is among the strongest predictors of whether patients reduce use.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
- Language shapes care: studies show even PhD-level clinicians recommend more punitive interventions for a "substance abuser" than for "a person with a substance use disorder," so Wakeman advocates person-first language and dropping terms like "clean" and "dirty"; she also cites Rat Park, positive childhood experiences (one caring adult reduces a traumatized child's addiction risk), and evidence that after five years of recovery a person's risk of addiction is no higher than the general public's — with 24 million Americans living in recovery.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]

# Takeaways

Wakeman's message is that addiction is a preventable, treatable, "good prognosis" illness whose casualties — including her own family member, Bartlett's friend Liam Payne, and patients she describes — die largely from stigma, punishment and missed medical moments rather than from any lack of effective tools.[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE] Her practical counsel: know the real risk thresholds and treat alcohol like any indulgence rather than a health behavior; anchor change in a personal "why" and structural changes rather than willpower; fill the space left by a substance with connection, purpose and healthier rewards; and approach people who are struggling with empathy and partnership — the upstream cure, she argues, is rebuilding resilience and community, since "the opposite of addiction is not sobriety, it's connection."[^alcohol-rewires-your-brain-dr-sarah-wakeman-Fg7U-BhiZGE]
