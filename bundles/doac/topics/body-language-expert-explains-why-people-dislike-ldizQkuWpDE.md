# Overview

Returning guest Vanessa Van Edwards — a self-described "recovering awkward person" who has taught communication to over 400,000 students — tells host Steven Bartlett that mastering communication "will get you everything you want": relationships, raises, friends, and even longevity[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE]. She argues most charisma is learned rather than innate, and walks through the mechanics: first impressions form the moment someone sees you (within 100 milliseconds for profile photos) and are about 76% accurate at guessing personality traits, so cues like visible hands, a broad posture, 60-70% eye contact, head tilts, authentic smiles, and "breaking the script" of small talk can be deliberately dialed up or down like a thermostat between warmth and competence[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].

The episode's centerpiece is a matchmaking experiment in an Austin bar, where Van Edwards coached first dates in real time through an earpiece. She was "shocked" that nearly every dater hid their true feelings — one woman who admitted she was attracted to her date came across as cold, gave dead-end answers, and declined a second date for being "too busy" — leading Van Edwards to declare that withholding our liking is "why we're having a baby crisis" and a root cause of loneliness[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE]. The conversation then covers the guest's chemical framing of conversation as "gifting" dopamine, serotonin, and testosterone; the seven universal micro expressions and how to respond to each; and the Big Five (OCEAN) personality traits, which she says are 42-57% heritable but leave roughly 30-40% room for deliberate change[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].

# Key points

- The guest says first impressions are lasting and about 76% accurate for personality traits, form the moment someone first sees you (not when you start talking), and happen within the first 100 milliseconds for profile pictures — but because different photos of the same person create different impressions, we can control the impression we make[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- Her "triple threat" for good first impressions is direct eye contact, visible hands, and a broad body; the worst mistakes are checking your phone (which mimics the universal defeat posture and makes you "accidentally look like a loser"), hidden hands, and avoidant gaze[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- She advises "breaking the script" of ritual small talk — answering "how are you?" with a number ("seven"), asking "what's good?" or "what are you looking forward to?" — and keeping a "story toolbox" note of prepared anecdotes for topics that always come up (weather, traffic, where you're from)[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- Master communicators, in her framing, "gift chemicals": dopamine (excitement), serotonin (calm and belonging, created by "me too" moments under her thread theory of conversation), and testosterone (feeling capable) — leaving people literally better than they were found[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- In the earpiece-coached dates, she diagnosed "dream-killing" (shutting down questions with flat answers like "I don't go out much"), numbers questions that dead-end conversation, closed body language producing unattractive vocal fry, and daters who never signal attraction — the woman who rejected a second date was, per Van Edwards, genuinely attracted but trapped by busyness, a checklist, and withheld warmth[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- She argues busyness "is not a mark of success," that dating checklists blind people to real connection, and that excess choice (the jam study) plus social-media-inflated attractiveness standards have left daters numb; her prescriptions for a funk include a 30-day no-mirror challenge and a social-media blackout to force in-person oxytocin[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- For leaders and professionals: answer "what do you do?" with "I am a [role] who helps [who]" plus hooks for follow-up questions, use "because" (citing the copy-machine study where any reason increased compliance), prime desired behavior with words like "team" and "collaboration," and on Zoom keep the camera on, be ready with an anecdote, and avoid fake backgrounds[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- The number one cause of loneliness, she claims, is that we withhold our liking; remedies include voicing affirmations in the moment ("this is so fun"), non-verbal liking cues (triple nods, head tilts, leans, easy laughter), and — citing research — women valuing humor in men while men mainly want a woman who laughs at their jokes[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- She teaches the seven universal micro expressions — fear (upper eye-whites showing, address the anxiety), disgust (crinkled nose, often leaks during lies), anger (hardened lips, jutted jaw, defuse with shared goals), authentic happiness (upper cheek muscles, unfakeable), sadness (most contagious), surprise (distinguish from fear to gauge if someone knew something), and contempt (a one-sided smirk)[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- On contempt she cites John Gottman's finding that observing couples on silent video predicts divorce within 30 years with 93.6% accuracy — contempt signals felt superiority, and she says only shared experience, not more talking, rebuilds the relationship[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- On the Big Five (OCEAN): traits are 42-57% heritable with roughly 30-40% room to shift; high-openness people carry the DRD4 gene variant giving extra dopamine from novelty; two low-conscientious partners have more financial problems; the best leaders skew high-extrovert and low-agreeable; and high neurotics like herself carry the short serotonin-transporter gene variant, producing less serotonin more slowly[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
- She says extroverts and the highly conscientious live longest (extroversion as "stress insurance" via support systems, with a cited 2015 study finding stronger immune responses in extroverts), while high neurotics have the shortest lifespans — "we worry ourselves to death" — and lifelong singles typically score low on openness, extraversion, and conscientiousness, a pattern she coaches around by anchoring socializing to loved activities[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].

# Takeaways

The guest's through-line is that connection is both a learnable skill and a neglected health behavior: warmth and competence cues can be practiced, scripts can be broken, and personality can be nudged 30-40% in a chosen direction — but none of it matters if people keep hiding that they like each other[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE]. She urges listeners to stop treating busyness and rigid routines as achievements, to show and speak their liking openly, and to invest in relationships as the strongest predictor of health, happiness, and a long life[^body-language-expert-explains-why-people-dislike-ldizQkuWpDE].
