# Overview

Behavioral investigator Vanessa Van Edwards, who describes herself as "a recovering awkward person," tells host Steven Bartlett that highly successful people speak a hidden language — the language of cues — and that failing to read or control those cues means missing a crucial element of success[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. Her central number, drawn from Susan Fiske's 2002 work, is that 82% of our impressions of people are based on warmth and competence, so a person who can consciously signal both is covering most of what others will conclude about them[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. She frames this as a thermostat rather than a personality type — five power cues to dial up competence, five warmth cues to dial up warmth, drawn from a set of 97 cues she has catalogued — and insists it works for introverts and ambiverts who have no wish to fake extroversion[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].

The origin story is a college group paper she begged to write alone, and a professor who told her the point was the people, not the paper, and suggested she study conversation the way she studied chemistry[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. Seventeen or eighteen years and 400,000 students later, she says her most dramatic transformations come from brilliant, stoic people who try to poker-face their way through interactions and end up in what she calls the danger zone — low warmth and low competence — where people cannot read or trust them, the failure mode she attributes to Ring founder Jamie Siminoff leaving *Shark Tank* without a deal on a billion-dollar idea[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].

A long middle stretch covers likeability, questions and relationships. Van Edwards argues likeability is in our control because the trait that separated popular high-schoolers in Van Sloan's research was simply having the longest list of people they liked, and she urges "aggressively liking" others, banning "what do you do?" for 30 days, and treating adult friendship like dating[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. She closes with warnings about ambivalent relationships being more draining than openly toxic ones, and about AirPods and hybrid work quietly destroying the weak ties and micro-moments from which friendships are built[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].

# Key points

- The guest says gestures beat words when the two conflict — the brain is "12.5 times more likely to believe my gesture over my words" — and demonstrates with an exercise (say "five" while holding up three fingers) to show why lying with gestures is hard and why liars use fewer of them[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Her team's coding of 2010 TED Talks found the most viral speakers used an average of 465 hand gestures in 18 minutes versus 271 for the least popular, and a test on her own YouTube channel found any hand gesture in a thumbnail increased clicks[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- She cites a study of 58,000 working hours across 11 companies finding that sitting within 25 feet of a high performer raises your own performance by 15%, while sitting within 25 feet of a low performer lowers it by 30% — evidence, she argues, that negative cues are more contagious[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Van Edwards describes a "cue cycle" in which a social rejection cue from someone else dilates the recipient's pupils and widens their field of vision into a fight-or-flight state, changing the cues they send back; citing Matthew Lieberman's fMRI work, she says silently labelling the cue you see ("lip purse," "noted") stops the amygdala activation and hands control back to you[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- On resting faces, she calls hers "resting bothered face," explains that downturned mouths read as sad, hooded eyes and vertical brow lines read as angry, and visible upper eye-whites read as fear; her three profile-picture mistakes are fear eyes, a one-sided contempt smirk (which via the facial feedback hypothesis makes you feel scornful too), and a fake smile[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Citing Barbara Wild's research, she says viewers of an authentic smile catch the happiness and show improved mood while viewers of a fake smile show no mood change — so she would rather people look neutral than fake-smile, and coaches Bartlett to activate the upper cheek muscles instead[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Her five power cues for competence are the steeple hand position (the highest-rated leader gesture, minus the "evil fingers" drumming), maximising the distance between earlobe and shoulder, eye contact concentrated at the end of sentences (60-70% of the conversation, since over 70% reads as territorial), the lower lid flex as a signal of focus, and the downward vocal inflection[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- She warns that the accidental question inflection flips listeners from listening to scrutinising, cites her lab's two-truths-and-a-lie experiments finding liars "ask" their lie, and says salespeople who ask their number are begging to be negotiated with; she also describes a study of warbled surgeon voice clips in which the doctors rated lowest on warmth and competence had the highest malpractice suit rates[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Her five warmth cues are the slow triple nod (which she says makes the other person speak 67% longer, but becomes "please wrap up" when sped up), the head tilt (which makes bad news land better), the authentic smile, the lean used as a highlight rather than continuously — too much becomes a submissive bow — and nonverbal bridges such as brief touches, reaches, or serving someone something[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- She maps Edward Hall's proxemic zones onto modern life, arguing the personal zone at roughly arm's length is where the best conversations happen, that a camera too close to your face accidentally signals intimacy on video calls, and that loud bars work romantically because they push strangers into each other's intimate zone[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Van Sloan's study of thousands of high-schoolers found the single variable predicting popularity was the length of a student's list of people they liked, plus constant micro-moments of greeting in hallways — which leads her to "be the first liker" and three magic phrases: "I was just thinking of you," "you're always so…," and "last time we talked you mentioned…," all aimed at countering signal amplification bias[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- She wants "what do you do?" retired for 30 days — it puts brains on autopilot and effectively asks what someone is worth — replaced with "working on anything exciting recently?", "what's your biggest goal right now?" (an "allergy question" that filters people fast), and the self-narrative probe "what book, movie or TV character is most like you, and why?", which she says revealed a six-year friend saw herself as Katniss Everdeen[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- She sketches three self-narratives — hero, healer and victim — and uses "do you feel lucky?" as a diagnostic, citing Richard Wiseman's newspaper study in which self-described lucky people spotted the planted notice saying there were 42 images while unlucky people kept counting and made more mistakes[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Also from Wiseman, she recounts the blender demonstration in which a model who spilled the smoothie sold more units, and a replication with a job candidate who spilled coffee and was rated better — the "other shoe effect," which she says argues for dropping a genuine imperfection early rather than performing perfection, as she did by opening *Captivate* with "I'm a recovering awkward person"[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- On attraction she cites Monica Moore's finding that the people approached most in clubs were not the most attractive but those signalling availability — open torso with nothing held in front of it, "croissant feet" angled toward the room, and repeated short glances, which by one count took around eight before someone approached[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- For lie detection she says the average person is about 54% accurate — barely better than a coin toss — so people should not overestimate themselves; the recurring statistical cues she watches for are the question inflection, a sudden volume drop, head-shake incongruence with spoken words (with a cultural caveat for India, Bulgaria and Pakistan), and flashes of self-disgust including nasal wing dilation, none of which prove lying but all of which mean "double-click"[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Citing research on police precincts, she argues ambivalent relationships — the ones where you are unsure whether you like each other — produce more workplace stress and less work-life balance than openly toxic ones, so people should deliberately move ambivalent contacts up or out[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
- Her closing example is Harry S. Truman, whom she describes as severely introverted, renting the one air-conditioned room in the basement of the 1944 Democratic National Convention and winning delegates one conversation at a time rather than competing on stage — her argument for building rooms that suit your social strength instead of faking extroversion[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].

# Takeaways

Van Edwards' argument is that charisma is a recipe rather than a gift — 97 cues from which each person assembles their own mix of warmth and competence, calibrated to whether they are currently read as intimidating or as not-to-be-taken-seriously[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. She is consistent that every cue can be overdone: too much competence reads as suspicious, too much warmth as unserious, too much lean as submission, too much nodding as impatience, and every cue works best as a highlight rather than a permanent setting[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw]. Her final prescriptions are behavioural and small — break conversational autopilot for 30 days, find where you sit on the warmth-competence grid and ask someone who knows you to rate you too, and treat friendship with the same intentionality as dating — on the grounds that the tools themselves generate the confidence to use them[^body-language-expert-stop-using-this-its-making-VHUrdELKjDw].
