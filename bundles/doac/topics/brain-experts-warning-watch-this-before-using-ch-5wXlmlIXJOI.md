# Overview

In this episode, host Steven Bartlett brings together psychiatrist and brain-imaging specialist Dr. Daniel Amen and computational neuroscientist Dr. Terry Sejnowski — who co-invented the Boltzmann machine with Geoffrey Hinton and presides over the foundation running the NeurIPS conference — to examine what large language models like ChatGPT are doing to human brains[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI]. The conversation is anchored by a widely publicized MIT study in which participants who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed a 47% collapse in brain activity and connectivity compared with unaided writers, could not reliably quote their own essays minutes later, and remained in cognitive "low gear" even when the tool was later removed — though Amen stresses the study is not yet peer reviewed[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].

Both guests converge on a "use it or lose it" framing: Amen argues that tools which reduce cognitive load may raise dementia risk over time, while Sejnowski counters with the calculator analogy — offloading can free the brain for higher work, but only if the user keeps interacting, questioning outputs, and thinking alongside the machine rather than deferring thinking to it[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI]. The discussion widens into AI companions such as Grok's flirtatious "Annie" character, which the guests say targets the limbic system and dopamine circuitry of lonely users and developing adolescent brains, and then into broader brain health: the guests cover SSRIs and benzodiazepines and dementia risk, childhood development, learning science (spaced repetition, sleep, procrastination), and Amen's "BRIGHT MINDS" risk-factor model[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI]. Bartlett closes by resolving to "do it the hard way" — using AI, but thinking and writing for himself whenever the thinking actually matters[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].

# Key points

- The host summarizes an MIT study of 54 participants from Boston-area universities writing essays in three groups (ChatGPT, Google, no tools): the ChatGPT group showed a 47% collapse in brain activity and connections, the weakest EEG activity, plunging memory scores, and little sense of ownership over their text, while the no-tool group lit up the widest neural networks[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Per the study as discussed, the ChatGPT group had nearly two times less activity in memory-linked brain regions, 83% of ChatGPT users could not correctly quote their own finished essay, and when forced to write unaided in a fourth session their brains stayed under-engaged — evidence, the host says, of lingering "cognitive debt"[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Amen emphasizes the study is not peer reviewed — its authors said they released it early because peer review can take six to eight months — but says it frightened him because lower cognitive load and less lifelong learning are associated with higher Alzheimer's risk, noting 50% of people 85 and older will be diagnosed with dementia[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Sejnowski cites education research (earlier Alzheimer's onset in the least-educated populations, later onset with more schooling) and the electronic-calculator precedent to argue benefits and risks must be weighed: misusing LLMs as a convenience will send your brain "downhill," but interacting with them — questioning outputs like you would a teacher — can build critical thinking[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- The guests link psychiatric drugs to dementia risk: a meta-analysis of five studies associating SSRIs with a 75% increased risk of dementia, a Swedish study of nearly 20,000 patients tying higher SSRI doses to faster cognitive decline (worst in men), and benzodiazepines' established association with dementia — alarming, Amen says, given 25% of American adults are on psychiatric drugs[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- On children, the guests cite figures that nearly 30% of US parents of kids aged 0-8 say their children use AI for learning and 54% of UK parents fear over-reliance; Amen warns weaker, less-engaged young brains are "much more likely to pick the one marshmallow," while Sejnowski allows that an AI trained to be a good teacher could scale one-on-one tutoring — though today's models lack basal ganglia, reinforcement-style value learning, and instilled cultural values[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Demonstrating Grok's "Annie" companion live, the guests describe such sexualized AI characters as engineered to activate the limbic system and dopamine while shutting down the prefrontal cortex — Amen compares it to Las Vegas casinos plying gamblers with alcohol and likens the mechanism to pornography's dopamine hijack in children; both are "horrified" at kids having access[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- On AI romance (surveys cited: 19% of Americans have interacted with AI romantic partners; 83% of Gen Z believe meaningful AI connection is possible), Sejnowski — speaking as a NeurIPS insider — dismisses much press coverage as misleading and predicts such attachments are transient, since the systems mimic emotion without having it; he adds a frictionless perfect partner removes the struggle and mood-management that actually develops the brain[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Sejnowski defends politeness toward chatbots: treating ChatGPT socially engages the brain's automatic social circuitry (one woman found it left her refreshed rather than exhausted), and he blasts Sam Altman's remark about "thank you" costing tens of millions of dollars, saying he wouldn't trust Altman because OpenAI optimizes profit, not users' health[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Amen invokes CDC data — 58% of teenage girls persistently sad, 32% having considered suicide, 13% having attempted it — to argue society unleashed phones and social media without neuroscience study and that AI is potentially far more dangerous because it is "way sexier"; he predicts unintended consequences within five years, not twenty or thirty[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- Practical guidance from the guests: use AI to amplify rather than replace thinking, alternate AI-assisted with brain-only tasks, ask it to test and critique you (as Bartlett does with his memos across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Grok), and preserve rote practice — the basal ganglia's repetition-based learning system that schools now wrongly disparage[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
- For general brain health the guests prescribe exercise ("by far the best drug"), sleep for memory consolidation, spaced repetition and the Ebbinghaus spacing effect, omega-3s, optimism, diaphragmatic breathing (twice as long out as in), and avoiding artificial sweeteners, chronic noise, GPS over-reliance (which weakens the hippocampus), and multitasking; Amen frames it all with one question — "is this good for my brain or bad for it?"[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].

# Takeaways

The guests' shared verdict is that AI itself is neither poison nor cure — the relationship determines the outcome. Amen warns that society has again "embraced convenience before understanding consequence," as with social media, and urges legislation, study, and brain-love education, while Sejnowski insists smart, interactive use can genuinely make people smarter. The host concludes that the trade-off of effortless AI is eroded critical thinking, and commits to deliberately "doing it the hard way" — thinking and writing for himself when it matters — as a hedge he believes will be a decisive advantage in the coming decade[^brain-experts-warning-watch-this-before-using-ch-5wXlmlIXJOI].
