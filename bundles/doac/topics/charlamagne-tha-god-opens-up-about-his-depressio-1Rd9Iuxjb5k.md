# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, host Steven Bartlett interviews Charlamagne tha God (Lenard McKelvey), co-host of the radio show The Breakfast Club, whom Bartlett introduces as America's most influential radio host, about his book *Get Honest or Die Lying*.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] The title, Charlamagne explains, is a play on 50 Cent's *Get Rich or Die Tryin'*, but he means it literally: he says a February spiritual retreat and an ayahuasca ceremony left him with one instruction — "stop lying to yourself and stop volunteering those lies to others" — and he argues most people do exactly that, feeding a version of themselves to social media that hides insecurity, fear and pain.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]

The conversation traces his life back to a single-wide trailer on a dirt road in Monks Corner, South Carolina.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He describes a father who preached "if you don't change your lifestyle you're going to end up in jail, dead, or broke" while himself selling drugs and having an affair, a sexual encounter at age eight with an older relative by marriage that he only later recognised as molestation, bullying that pushed him from an honours class into being the class clown, and an arrest tied to a shooting that put him in jail for roughly 45 days as a teenager.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He tells Bartlett that the through-line from that childhood is people-pleasing, anxiety, impostor syndrome and depression — and that he could not name any of it until therapy, which he began around 2015-2016, roughly five years into the success of The Breakfast Club.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]

The second half of the episode is Charlamagne's case that external success fixes nothing internally, that role models are misread as their possessions, and that social media is an abusive relationship people have voluntarily entered.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He argues that purpose arrives only through service to others, pointing to his podcast network, his scholarship fund, and a nonprofit whose stated goal is free therapy for 10,000 Black and brown people.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]

# Key points

- Charlamagne says the phrase that reorganised his life came out of an ayahuasca ceremony earlier that year: stop lying to yourself, and stop volunteering those lies to other people — a habit he thinks social media industrialises by demanding you "feed the beast" daily.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- He describes his father as absent in the ways that mattered, and says he only learned in 2018 — the week a 24-25-year-old cousin died by suicide on his fourth attempt, and after his father read *Shook One* — that his father had attended therapy two or three times a week, had attempted suicide 30-plus years earlier, and had been on 10 to 12 psychiatric medications.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- Confronted about cheating on his mother, his father asked him "so you only got one woman, huh?", which Charlamagne says pushed him into years of trying to prove he was a player — only for his father to later tell him that leaving his mother was one of the worst mistakes he ever made and that his son "always had it right".[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- At eight, a much older female relative by marriage initiated sexual contact and called him ugly — telling people his nose was swollen, to the point his grandmother applied cream to it — and he says he kept complying so she would stop, which he traces directly to his adult people-pleasing.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He says he and several friends joked about such "encounters" for years and only recognised it as molestation after watching Tyler Perry cry about his own abuse on The Oprah Winfrey Show.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- He offers his father slapping him at the roadside — after telling him to "do what I do" and then running a stop sign onto Highway 52 — as his defining example of being disciplined for things he was never taught, which he names in therapy as a root of his insecurity and impostor syndrome.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- On male role models, he argues the term is misused: because you can only model what a person shows you, admiring someone online means copying "the man's things" — the Lamborghini, the Rolex — not his morals, so "you might as well be modelling a mannequin"; the only person you will ever truly know, he says, is yourself.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- His career was 12 years of rejection — a 1998 radio internship, on air by 1999, four firings from four stations, plus jobs in telemarketing, a warehouse, a flower garden and Taco Bell before joining The Breakfast Club at 32 in 2010 — and he dates his first panic attack to his first day of first grade and his first major one to driving down I-26 after his fourth firing, when a doctor told him his heart was fine — "you have an athlete's heart" — and that he was describing anxiety; he assumed a new job would fix it, and says three or four years into unprecedented success and money at The Breakfast Club nothing had changed.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- He is candid that suicidal thoughts still surface unprompted — "what am I still doing here" — and describes the death of his friend Jazz Waters during Covid, with whom he had three- and four-hour Sunday phone calls about therapy and depression, and hearing her voice ask whether he was still here.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] Asked what he is most afraid of feeling, he answers grief — specifically the grief of death, having not yet lost a parent, spouse or child.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- He attributes his on-air ruthlessness to fear rather than confidence: he had just collected his last unemployment cheque and was living at his mother's house, so "everything you saw was rooted in I'm not going back to that", including pain he was projecting onto guests — and being rewarded for it fuelled the behaviour until 2015, when he saw himself about to repeat his father's infidelity and lose the wife he has been with for 26 years.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- His healing toolkit is deliberately broad — therapy, meditation, breathwork, reiki, crystals and plant medicine — and he notes a large racial disparity in who seeks mental health care, saying he first even heard of therapy from the TV show *Girlfriends* and later from Neal Brennan, Pete Davidson and Devi Brown.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He also credits his father's belated honesty in 2018 with letting him finally see him as a man doing his best without the tools or resources his son now has.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- He argues that people with high book intelligence but low emotional IQ are losing themselves to social media, crafting opinions to please online crowds — he cites deliberately ignoring people trying to shape his take on a new Kendrick Lamar record — and that "you can't grow when you're watering somebody else's garden".[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
- Asked how he is doing, he says purpose comes through service, quoting Wayne Dyer's *The Power of Intention*: he runs the Black Effect podcast network of around 30 shows, flies 50 HBCU students to Nashville for his Thrill of Possibility Summit, donated a quarter of a million dollars to South Carolina State University in his mother's name, and runs the Mental Wealth Alliance with a goal of free therapy for 10,000 Black and brown people over five years.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]

# Takeaways

Charlamagne's argument, as he puts it, is that the mask is the illness: the drug-selling, the roster of women, the on-air cruelty and eventually the panic attacks were all versions of a country kid managing fear he had never been given language for, and no amount of external success touched any of it.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] What did move, in his telling, was naming things — therapy, honest conversations with his father, refusing to let online crowds author his opinions — and then converting the resources that came with fame into service, which he now describes as the only thing large enough to be worth building.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k] He closes on the book's second theme, why small talk sucks: he suggests social media has trained people to treat micro concerns as macro ones, and that the fulfilling conversations are the uncomfortable, big ones most men are never taught to have.[^charlamagne-tha-god-opens-up-about-his-depressio-1Rd9Iuxjb5k]
