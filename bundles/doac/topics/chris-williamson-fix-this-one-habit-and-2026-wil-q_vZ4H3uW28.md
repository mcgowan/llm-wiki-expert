# Overview

In this end-of-year episode, Modern Wisdom host Chris Williamson joins Steven Bartlett to argue that the week between Christmas and New Year is not magically special but is a culturally and practically convenient moment for structured reflection — people are already ruminating about the past and worrying about the future, so an annual review simply channels that into deliberate planning[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28]. Bartlett frames the problem with the failure statistics of resolutions: he cites figures that 23% of people quit by the end of the first week of January, roughly half quit by the end of January, and only about 9% keep a resolution for the full year[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28]. Williamson's central corrective is subtraction before addition: "in order to pick something up, you have to put something down" — you should assume you can do no more than you are doing now, pick at most two big goals for the year, and never rely on motivation as a fuel source you don't control[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].

The conversation then works through Williamson's highest-return habits and reflection questions — no phone in the bedroom (which he calls an instant 15% quality-of-life increase), a morning walk, delaying caffeine 90 minutes after waking, a six-month alcohol break, a ten-minute post-meal walk, and James Clear's "never miss two days in a row" rule — with both hosts arguing that sleep is the first domino from which most other behavior change flows[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28]. Williamson also unpacks his signature concepts: the "lonely chapter" of personal growth, the region beta paradox (comfortable mediocrity is more dangerous than outright badness), productivity dysmorphia and "productivity debt", observable versus hidden metrics of success, and the "unteachable lesson" that external accolades don't fill internal voids[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].

The episode turns unexpectedly personal near the end: Williamson reveals that toxic mold poisoning derailed the past two years of his life, attacking his energy, mood, and cognition — the exact traits his career depends on — and that his only goals for the year were "don't let the show drop and fix my health"[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28]. Both hosts also discuss fertility decline, why top podcasters don't have kids (Bartlett says he expects to be a father within twelve months), and their frustration with British tall-poppy culture after a newspaper criticized the three Brits in Spotify's global top-ten podcasts[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].

# Key points

- The guest argues New Year is not intrinsically special, but the Christmas-to-New-Year lull is a "culturally appropriate moment" for structured reflection, since people are already reflecting and planning in an unstructured way anyway[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- The host cites resolution statistics: 23% of people quit within the first week of January, about half quit by the end of January, and only around 9% keep their resolution for a full year[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- Williamson's core rule is subtraction before addition — "in order to pick something up, you have to put something down" — and he says you can realistically achieve only about two big goals in a year; overloading the plate near-guarantees failure[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- His single best planning question: "What would have to happen by the end of 2026 for me to look back and consider it a success?" — plus reflection prompts like "How would I spend my day to make 85-year-old me as miserable as possible?" and "If your life was a movie, what would the audience be screaming at the screen?"[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- He calls charging your phone outside the bedroom "an instant 15% quality of life increase" and his highest-ROI resolution, improving sleep, mornings, and presence; other high-return habits include a morning walk, no caffeine within 90 minutes of waking, a six-month alcohol break with an accountability buddy, and a ten-minute postprandial walk to regulate glucose[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- From James Clear's Atomic Habits, he keeps one rule above all: never miss two days in a row — "one missed day is an error, two missed days is the start of a new habit" — and in 2018 he replaced a failed "gym every day" resolution with an incompletable goal of "consistency in the gym"[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- Both hosts argue sleep is the foundational first domino: caffeine, evening phone use, and alcohol all degrade it, and "the problem might be hiding at the bottom of the glass" — Bartlett recounts how two glasses of wine after a year sober ruined three days of his life via a domino effect on sleep, diet, and training[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- Williamson describes "productivity dysmorphia" and "productivity debt" — waking up already behind, where a perfect day only gets you back to a draw — and argues type-A "insecure overachievers" need a "parasympathetic Goggins" telling them to rest, not more hustle content[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- He explains procrastination as one of two fixable failures — not knowing the next physical action (David Allen's Getting Things Done) or not knowing how to do it — and both discuss Jordan Peterson's story of a shut-in whose recovery began with embarrassingly small steps like plugging in a vacuum cleaner[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- The region beta paradox: situations that are "not bad enough to be bad, but nowhere near good enough to be good" trap people in comfortable complacency, whereas worse circumstances would galvanize change; Bartlett adds Obama's 51%-certainty decision threshold and Bezos's reversible type-two doors[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- The "lonely chapter" names the stage of growth where you've outgrown old friends but haven't yet built new ones — Williamson insists the doubt and discomfort are a feature, not a bug, of real change, and quotes Ryan Holiday: "Self-belief is overrated. Generate evidence."[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- On fertility decline, he cites demographers Lyman Stone and Stephen J. Shaw, notes South Korea's trajectory implies four great-grandchildren per hundred South Koreans (a 96% reduction), and contrasts K-pop's celibacy norms with a Georgian pastor whose offer to personally baptize every family's third child boosted births[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- Williamson discloses a two-year battle with toxic mold poisoning that hit his energy, mood, and cognition — at one point he forgot how to tie his shoes — reframing his year around "boring victories" and the realization that "suppression isn't the same thing as strength"[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
- On dating, he recommends asking "Am I the sort of person that the person I want to date would want to date?", names the gym as the single decision that most increased his attractiveness, and advises partner selection around psychological stability — how fast someone returns to emotional baseline — plus conscientiousness, moderate agreeableness, and moderate openness[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].

# Takeaways

The guest's closing message is anti-deferral: reject the "provisional life" in which real living is always about to begin, because problems are a feature of life that never go away, and "no one is getting out of this game alive"[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28]. He argues the striver's path must eventually swap toxic fuel (chips on shoulders, need for validation) for self-compassion, and he offers his free annual review template of reflection questions as the practical vehicle — ending with the claim that agency, the belief you can impact your surroundings, is the most important component of human joy to preserve[^chris-williamson-fix-this-one-habit-and-2026-wil-q_vZ4H3uW28].
