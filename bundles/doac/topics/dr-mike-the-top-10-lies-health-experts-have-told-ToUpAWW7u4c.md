# Overview

Dr Mike Varshavski is a practising family physician at a community health centre — where he says half the patients have no insurance — who also runs one of the largest health education channels on the internet.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c] He tells Steven Bartlett that his mission is to make sure that in a sea of misinformation his patients and viewers get honest, transparent and engaging information, and that he came to it after watching patients turn to him as a medical student and ask "what did he say?" the moment a world-class specialist left the room — proof, he argues, that doctors are poor communicators.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c] His entry into social media was accidental: a BuzzFeed article about "this sexy doctor and his awesome dog" went viral, and he decided to use the attention as a delivery mechanism for accurate information, borrowing the marketing techniques that snake-oil sellers had used so effectively.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]

His central diagnostic idea is what he calls the "grey zone" — the space where modern science does not yet have an answer, and where sellers rush in with certainty.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c] Thirty years ago, he says, the grey zone was weight loss, filled with late-night fat-burner and grapefruit-diet ads; now that GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic have produced real FDA-approved results, the grey zone has migrated to anti-aging and longevity, a field he argues has been corrupted the way the rest of healthcare has been corrupted by capitalism.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c] The tell, in his framing, is confidence itself: "the confidence is the misinformation," because real science answers most questions with "I don't know," "it depends," and "for whom?"[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]

The conversation also covers his mother's death from an aggressive form of CLL during medical school — he was in the room asking doctors to stop chest compressions — the isolation and depression that followed, and how a discounted boxing class became a ten-year career culminating in a Showtime pay-per-view fight in front of 15,000 people.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c] The episode ends with him teaching Bartlett hands-only CPR on a mannequin.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]

# Key points

- On dieting, he says two things must be held together: calories in and calories out is true, however "clinical and cold and heartless" it sounds, and the nutrient content of the food matters — a grapefruit diet works on the first axis and fails on the second.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He defends his own "buzzy" statement that exercise almost doesn't matter for weight loss, arguing weight change comes overwhelmingly from nutrition — most people are not physically equipped to burn the 1,000-plus calories in a cinema trip of popcorn, candy and soda — while insisting exercise's benefits are "literally everything else": mood, social connection, strength, reduced cancer risk, longevity.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He cites general guidelines of 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise a week, defining moderate intensity as being able to manage only about one sentence at a time before running out of breath.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He notes that for essentially every chronic disease — high blood pressure, diabetes — the first-line treatment doctors are actually taught is lifestyle modification, contrary to the assumption that medicine reaches for drugs first.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On supplements he argues the evidence for almost all of them is missing absent a specific medical reason; that they carry three distinct harms — displacing things that actually work, consuming a limited health budget, and having real side effects and drug interactions; and that excess of even "healthy" B vitamins and antioxidants can become pro-inflammatory, which nobody discusses because it cannot be sold.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He points out supplements are not FDA-regulated, that Consumer Reports testing has found products missing their listed ingredients or containing 5–10x the labelled dose of things like fat-soluble vitamin A, and that he and Bartlett could bottle anything on the table and sell it legally so long as they used buzzwords like "immunity support" rather than naming a condition.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On the microbiome he draws a careful line: we know fibre consumption correlates with better outcomes and that disease states alter gut bacteria, but we do not know that a prebiotic supplement reproduces the fibre benefit or that reintroducing a bacterium in pill form fixes a condition — and microbiome tests are too preliminary to act on.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He explains why animal research should not be extrapolated: roughly 99% of what pharmaceutical companies pursue from promising animal studies fails, and of what survives into human trials another 99% fails.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On vaping, he argues it is a legitimate tool for quitting cigarettes but carries no health value for someone who never smoked; the danger is that being less offensive in odour and easier to conceal makes it easier to take bigger doses and hook adolescents whose frontal lobes are still developing. He notes vaping-related lung injury now has its own diagnosis code.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He offers a counterintuitive pandemic argument: the more lethal a pathogen, the less damage it often does. Original SARS was far more lethal than SARS-CoV-2, but asymptomatic spread meant COVID-19 killed millions more — his example of why "insidious" misinformation and complacency outperform obvious threats.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On trust, he argues that Western medicine's willingness to call out its own failures — opioids, pharmaceutical pricing, past vaccine incidents — is its strength, and that the correct response to fraud in one drug decades ago is not to discard the whole field. He also notes Tylenol/paracetamol-induced liver failure drives huge numbers of hospitalisations yet triggers no outrage, because it is not emotionally charged the way birth control is.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- He singles out Gary Brecka's claim that headaches are caused by low pink Himalayan sea salt as an example of unfalsifiable overconfidence, noting he cannot disprove such claims without running his own study, but that they fail to cohere with established evidence.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On depression and grief he offers the advice he used himself — action precedes motivation, so put your shoes on and go anywhere — crediting Barbara Corcoran for the shoes-by-the-bed version, and describing a dog park as the most effective therapy he has had.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
- On CPR he stresses it does not restart the heart; it circulates residual oxygen to buy time until responders arrive. Call for help first by pointing at a specific person, push two inches (5cm) deep in the centre of the chest using body weight not triceps, swap out every two minutes, and note that women receive worse bystander CPR because people hesitate to push between the breasts. He also recounts improvising a cardiac-arrest dose of epinephrine mid-Atlantic for a passenger in anaphylaxis — a story that led to Chuck Schumer's office campaigning for EpiPens on planes.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]

# Takeaways

Varshavski's through-line is that healthcare resists the shortcuts that work in business and communication, and that the goal should be "good, not perfect" — because chasing perfection produces both physiological harm and the anxiety he describes battling himself over social media comments. He is explicit that he does not always follow his own advice, calling doctors "probably the biggest hypocrites when it comes to their own health," and he argues the expert worth following is the one willing to say what they do not know. His policy wish, echoing Jonathan Haidt, is to get phones out of schools and to think about age-gating social media the way society already gates cigarettes and alcohol.[^dr-mike-the-top-10-lies-health-experts-have-told-ToUpAWW7u4c]
