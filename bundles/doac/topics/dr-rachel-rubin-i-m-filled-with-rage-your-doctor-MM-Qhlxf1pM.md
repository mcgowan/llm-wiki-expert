# Overview

Dr. Rachel Rubin, a urologist and sexual medicine specialist practicing in Washington D.C. and Los Angeles, tells Steven Bartlett she is "filled with rage" because women are denied basic information and cheap, safe treatments for hormonal and sexual health — not out of malice, but because "everyone forgot to teach your doctor." She argues the failure is a great equalizer: Melinda Gates saw three doctors before getting proper hormone therapy, Oprah saw five without her palpitations being linked to perimenopause, and Halle Berry was misdiagnosed with herpes when she had genitourinary syndrome of menopause. She notes the word "clitoris" does not even appear in the training checklist for OB/GYNs, and that most doctors, working in 10-minute appointments they were never trained for, say "no" rather than "I don't know."[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]

The heart of the episode is Rubin's reframing of hormones. She describes menopause as a "castration event" — estrogen effectively going to zero, affecting bones, brain, heart, bladder, and sexual function — and points out that testosterone, wrongly considered a male hormone, begins declining in women's 30s, often taking libido, arousal, and orgasm with it. She lays out a four-bucket toolbox: systemic estrogen, progesterone, testosterone, and low-dose vaginal hormones, arguing the 2002 Women's Health Initiative press conference wrongly demonized hormone therapy for a generation and that only 1.7% of eligible women now receive prescriptions. Her strongest advocacy is for vaginal estrogen or DHEA — cheap, safe at any age and with any medical history, and proven since the 1990s to cut urinary tract infections by more than half, yet unprescribed for over 75% of the women who could benefit.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]

The conversation then turns to the orgasm gap, painful sex, pelvic floor health, pornography, and communication. Rubin argues most female sexual "dysfunction" is actually an education problem — women told they are broken because they don't orgasm from penetration, when the clitoris (a largely internal, penis-equivalent structure) is how most women orgasm — and that couples who learn the biology, drop the search for a "bad guy," and talk about sex outside the bedroom can fix problems they assumed were unfixable.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]

# Key points

- Rubin says the word "clitoris" is absent from the 2026 OB/GYN training checklist, that gynecologists receive almost no education in sexual pain, libido, arousal, or orgasm, and that she hands patients a mirror during exams because most women have never been taught the names of their own anatomy.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- She frames menopause as a castration event — if a man's testicles were removed he would get hot flashes, osteoporosis, depression, low libido, and metabolic syndrome — yet while medicine treats castrated men aggressively, every woman over ~50 loses her estrogen and is largely left untreated.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Testosterone in women falls precipitously starting in the 30s (not at menopause), and Rubin says there is global consensus it helps libido in postmenopausal women, plus data for arousal, orgasm, satisfaction, and body image; she prescribes FDA-approved male testosterone at about one-tenth the male dose, with effects taking 3–6 months.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Combined birth control pills shut down the ovaries — which normally make estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone — but only replace the first two, which Rubin says explains libido loss in a subset of users; Bartlett cites studies where up to 27% of pill users report reduced sex drive.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Rubin's unpublished survey of 1,000 women on GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (presented at a medical conference) found about 25% reported sexual side effects — roughly half of those saying function worsened — while noting there is not a single published paper on women's sexual side effects of these drugs.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- She recounts that the Women's Health Initiative was misreported at its 2002 press conference as showing hormone therapy causes heart disease and breast cancer; the same authors published in 2025 that below age 70 that older form of therapy carried no increased cardiovascular or stroke risk — yet only 1.7% of eligible women get hormone therapy prescriptions today.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Her four-bucket hormone toolbox: systemic estrogen (hot flashes, sleep, bone), progesterone (uterine protection, sleep, anxiety), testosterone (libido), and vaginal hormones — microdosed estradiol cream ($14 on Mark Cuban's pharmacy), tablets, a 3-month ring, or vaginal DHEA (Intrarosa) — used twice weekly.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- She calls vaginal hormones "literally better than Viagra" for arousal and orgasm and, as a urologist, stresses they prevent UTIs — which can escalate to urosepsis and death in older women — by more than half, are safe at any age and after cancer, clot, or stroke, yet fewer than 9% of Medicare patients and under 25% of eligible women get them; common advice like peeing after sex and wiping front-to-back she dismisses as folklore.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Rubin rejects waiting for ovarian failure before treating: "We don't make you go blind completely before we give you eyeglasses" — treatment should begin when symptoms begin, whether in perimenopause (roughly ages 35–45, before an average menopause at 52), postpartum/breastfeeding (a menopause-like hormone crash she calls genitourinary syndrome of lactation), or later; she illustrates with a patient in her 60s who, after stepwise vaginal hormones, estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone, enrolled in law school and finished top of her class.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- On the orgasm gap: about 20% of women report being unable to orgasm, most women do not orgasm from penetration because the clitoris — a large, mostly internal structure identical to penile tissue with ~10,000 nerve endings — is the primary organ of female orgasm; men average about 5.5 minutes to orgasm with penetration while women typically need well over 13–15 minutes of mostly clitoral stimulation, which is why she suspects many "clockwork" orgasms are faked and argues the woman should often orgasm first.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- She reports that about 23% of women have clitoral adhesions (the hood stuck to the glans), and her published data show a simple office procedure to remove them improved orgasm, arousal, and satisfaction by up to 60–70% — yet virtually no doctor ever examines the clitoris.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
- Up to 75% of women experience painful sex at some point (10–20% chronically, rising sharply at menopause); Rubin insists sex is not supposed to hurt, and causes span hormonally sensitive skin, tight pelvic floor muscles, nerves, and endometriosis scarring — she recommends specialized clinicians and pelvic floor physical therapy, and counters the "spontaneity myth" by advocating scheduled intimacy, quarterly partner days, and talking about sex outside the bedroom.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]

# Takeaways

Rubin's core message is that "biology matters in women": problems routinely written off as psychological, relational, or simply aging — low libido, painful sex, absent orgasms, recurring UTIs, "not feeling like myself" — often have identifiable hormonal and anatomical causes with cheap, safe, evidence-backed treatments that doctors were never taught to prescribe. Bartlett connects this personally, recounting how an ex-partner's undiagnosed physiological issue was misread as personal failure, and both conclude that education plus communication — approaching problems as "me and her against the problem" rather than hunting for a bad guy — is what turns fixable biology into saved relationships. These are Rubin's clinical views and advocacy positions, delivered with the explicit aim of pushing patients to advocate for themselves and doctors to learn the prescriptions.[^dr-rachel-rubin-i-m-filled-with-rage-your-doctor-MM-Qhlxf1pM]
