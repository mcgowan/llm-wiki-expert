# Overview

In this Diary Of A CEO debate episode, host Steven Bartlett moderates a heated exchange between Gary Stevenson — a former Citibank interest-rates trader from a poor East London family who says he was Citibank's most profitable trader in the world in 2011, making the bank over $35 million by betting the economy would keep getting worse — and Daniel Priestley, an Australian-born serial entrepreneur who grew up in a town with 24% unemployment and now runs a group of seven companies. Both agree Western living standards are deteriorating and the middle class is being hollowed out, but they diagnose opposite causes.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]

Stevenson argues that living standards are falling because of growing wealth inequality: pandemic-era deficits (roughly £1 trillion in the UK, trillions more in the US) amounted to a massive transfer of wealth from governments and ordinary families to the rich, and unless the very wealthy — he proposes taxing wealth above £10 million — are taxed more, the asset-owning class will squeeze ordinary people out of housing and their children will live in poverty. He predicts inequality of the kind seen in South Africa, Nigeria, or India is "the future of this country," and says he has been betting on it profitably for 15 years.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]

Priestley counters that the culprit is shrinking economic freedom: big government (UK state spending at 45% of GDP by his figures), record taxes, regulation, an outdated schooling system, and technology and remote work moving jobs offshore. He argues wealth is now intangible and mobile — the "Magnificent Seven" US tech companies grew from about $5 trillion to $17 trillion in value through the pandemic — so wealth taxes simply drive millionaires abroad, citing an estimated 10,800 high-net-worth individuals leaving the UK in 2024 (one every 45 minutes). The debate closes with sharply contrasting advice to young viewers and a disagreement over whether Trump-era tax-cutting or wealth redistribution will deliver better outcomes.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]

# Key points

- Stevenson recounts that after the 2008 crash, elite economists and traders wrongly predicted rate rises year after year; by betting in 2011 that the economy would stay weak "forever," he became Citibank's most profitable trader globally, making just over $35 million that year.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Stevenson argues COVID lockdowns plus roughly £1 trillion of UK deficit spending (about £20,000 per UK adult, with US figures around $13-14 trillion) funneled money from government through workers' bills to the rich — a wealth transfer he says nobody in politics, media, or academia even thought to question.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Priestley's framework is the Economic Freedom Index: he claims the UK fell from 82/100 in 2006 to 68/100, and that low-freedom countries see poverty jump from under 10% to 30% or more, while citing India's progress against extreme poverty as freedom increased.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Priestley says the pandemic moved the economy digital: the Magnificent Seven US tech firms went from about $5 trillion to $17 trillion in valuation, and technology makes jobs simpler, global, and ultimately automated away.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- On housing, Priestley blames government-inflated debt and a "housing traffic jam" — he claims 9.5 million UK homes have two or more spare bedrooms and 78% of housing wealth is held by baby boomers — while Stevenson insists inflated asset values inevitably transfer wealth from young workers to existing asset owners.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- The host cites comparative statistics: US GDP per head of $82,000 versus $49,000 in the UK, US income per head up 72% since 2007 versus down 2% in the UK in dollar terms, and US productivity up about 30% since 2018 — Priestley credits US tech and markets, while Stevenson notes the deficit-running US state outperformed austerity Britain.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- The two clash over the Duke of Westminster: Stevenson says the Duke inherited around £10 billion while paying trust "periodic taxes" of 6% every ten years (0.6% a year) versus the roughly 60% marginal rates Stevenson paid on earnings; Priestley replies that inheritance is not income, that the trust structure is available to anyone, and that the Duke is among the UK's largest taxpayers and employers.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Priestley cites the "millionaire exodus": about 1,000 millionaires a month leaving the UK — 10,800 high-net-worth individuals in 2024, a 57% year-on-year rise — after the non-dom regime ended, and notes the top 1% pay about 30% of UK income tax and the top 10% about 60%, so departures shift the burden onto everyone else.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Stevenson counters that a 1% wealth tax above £10 million is marginal and modest (roughly £20 billion by his back-of-napkin estimate), that taxing the rich is hard but necessary — as post-war Britain proved when high taxes on the wealthy funded healthcare, education, and housing — and that the post-war "golden age" is the only period in history that delivered broad living standards to ordinary people.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- The debate's most heated moment comes over agency: Priestley argues it has never been cheaper to start a business, that 65% of ultra-high-net-worth individuals are self-made, and that victim mindset causes mental illness; Stevenson retorts that he is "sick of multi-millionaires telling kids who can't afford to turn the heating on you just need to be more entrepreneurial," recalling a man in Newcastle who cried because "nobody ever told me that it wasn't my fault before."[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Stevenson claims his own path is not replicable — he cites his hard-working sister who can barely pay rent — and warns UK poverty could reach 95-98% within 70 years if wealth extraction from the middle class is not stopped; his prescription is political pressure ("get 5 million followers and bully the politicians") to tax wealth-hoarders and cut taxes on working people.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
- Priestley's closing prediction is that Trump's low-tax, pro-entrepreneur approach will attract the world's wealthy to the US and raise American living standards at the expense of the countries they leave; both agree voters should be swing voters rather than treating politics "like football."[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]

# Takeaways

The episode is less a resolution than a mapping of two rival diagnoses of Western decline. Both guests agree that the middle class is collapsing, that housing is unaffordable, that global tech firms shift profits abroad unfairly, and that young people face worse prospects than their parents — but Stevenson locates the cause in wealth inequality and prescribes taxing large fortunes to stop the rich absorbing society's assets, while Priestley locates it in big government and lost economic freedom and prescribes deregulation, lower taxes, and mass entrepreneurship. Their advice to individuals diverges accordingly: Priestley urges viewers to exploit the booming digital economy and take personal agency; Stevenson urges them to work hard but also to recognize the game is rigged, prepare for worse, and fight politically as their grandparents did. These are the guests' contested positions, aired as debate rather than settled fact.[^emergency-debate-they-lied-about-the-economy-rec-4yohVh4qcas]
