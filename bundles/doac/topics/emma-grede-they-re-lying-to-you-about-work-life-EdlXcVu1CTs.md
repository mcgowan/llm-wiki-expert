# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Emma Grede, the East London-born co-founder of Good American (with Khloé Kardashian) and founding partner of Skims, about the mindset and mechanics behind her rise from a working-class childhood to multi-billion-dollar brand builder. Grede traces her character to being the eldest of four girls raised by a single mother in Plaistow, where she ironed school shirts, made packed lunches, and felt the weight of unpaid bills from a young age — an upbringing she credits with instilling the grit, responsibility, and "I'll do that" attitude she considers the single trait without which she would not have succeeded.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]

The episode's most provocative thread is Grede's stance on work-life balance: she argues it is the employee's problem to solve, not the employer's responsibility, and calls asking about it in a job interview an immediate red flag. She insists that while an ordinary good life should not require working evenings and weekends, anyone claiming you can reach the very top while keeping all of them is lying — "extraordinary effort" is coupled to an extraordinary life.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]

The conversation also covers the origin story of Good American — born from her frustration at arranging talent-equity deals she couldn't share in — her philosophy on hiring, firing, and "enterprise mentality" leadership, her skepticism of manifestation culture and "toxic positivity" aimed at women, and a candid account of her fertility journey through IVF, three pregnancy losses, and twins born via surrogacy, ending with the launch of her own podcast, Aspire.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]

# Key points

- The guest names grit as her defining trait: despite being dyslexic and a self-described "serial dropout" (leaving school at 15 and later college), she says she has "zero qualifications" for being an apparel CEO, raising hundreds of millions, or starting agencies in multiple countries — she simply prepares, sees the opportunity, and makes it happen.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- On visualization: she repeatedly drew her dream living room as a child and decades later realized her Clerkenwell townhouse matched the drawing — but she warns against "toxic positivity" in manifestation culture, insisting that beneath any vision board must sit enormous work, planning, focus (a "force multiplier"), and the ability to drown out noise.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- She distinguishes decision feedback from customer feedback: for big decisions she calls seven or eight informed people but ultimately "calls the play" from her gut, whereas "everything your customer says is true because it's true to them."[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- A formative moment came when her husband Jens told her, just before an early board meeting at her agency ITB, "You have an employee mentality" — seeking the board's approval instead of leading them — which she calls one of the biggest unlocks of her career.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- She argues against hunting for mentors: good mentors don't have time to mentor you, so instead be relentlessly inquisitive and treat whoever is around you as one — she copied down her boss's sales-call lines and reused them on her own calls the same day.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- On work-life balance, she says asking about it in an interview means "something is wrong with you"; host Bartlett notes his own screening survey shows roughly 33% of the public rank work-life balance among their most important job factors; Grede counters that balance and profitability correlate — employers can only offer flexibility if the business wins — and describes work as a mutual "social contract."[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- She is blunt that being "number one" while keeping all evenings and weekends is impossible ("tell me who she is and I'll show you a liar"), and laments remote work's hidden costs: she guarantees she would not be where she is had she worked from home in her 20s, because observational learning, speed, and relationships happen in person.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- Her promotion criteria: be "120%" excellent in your current role before asking for the next one; she hires attitude over experience, prizes T-shaped people curious about the whole business, and demands flexibility even from 20-year veterans because technology keeps changing the customer.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- Her top hiring advice is "learn to fire": the people who get you to 10 million are not the people who get you to 100 million, and her early failure to move people out restricted her growth; she now spends 20-25% of her time on talent and defines culture as "who you hire, who you fire, and who gets promoted."[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- Good American's origin: after brokering flat-fee celebrity-equity deals (watching value she created accrue to others), she resolved to found her own company and bring talent in as equity partners — pitching Kris Jenner and then Khloé Kardashian on a denim brand offering all 19 sizes with no separate plus-size section, an instinct she says proved "100% right" because underserved customers simply had nothing to buy.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- She credits board members Andrew Rosen and John Howard for insisting on profitability over growth-at-all-costs during the 2018 era of cheap social-media customer acquisition — a "three-year golden period" she says is gone — and now bets on in-person, experiential marketing, citing a Skims diner pop-up on Sunset whose six days of 24-hour slots booked out within five minutes.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- On prejudice, she acknowledges stereotype threat is real (Bartlett cites studies where priming race or gender before tests lowered performance) but says being the only Black woman in the room often worked to her advantage in London, adding that race is experienced "very, very differently" in America; she refuses to internalize others' prejudice — "it doesn't have to be your problem."[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
- Her fertility story: effortless pregnancies at 30 and 33, then unexplained infertility at 38, multiple IVF rounds, and losses at 9, 11, and 16 weeks during COVID, before her twins were born via a surrogate she describes with deep gratitude; she urges listeners to actually plan for children because "the window is really narrow" and egg freezing is not the guarantee people assume.[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]

# Takeaways

Grede's through-line is personal responsibility paired with honesty about cost: success is available to people with no credentials, but only through grit, focus, in-person hustle, and treating work-life balance as your own problem to engineer rather than a perk to demand. She applies the same candor to leadership (you cannot be a people-pleaser and a leader; put the enterprise first), to hiring (obsess over talent, and fire fast), and to womanhood (reject toxic positivity, talk openly about fertility and its narrow window). Her closing reflection is that being deeply loved — by her mother, sisters, husband, and children — is what gives her ever-growing capacity to work, give, and take risks, and her new podcast Aspire is framed as a way to scale that mentorship, particularly for women who, like her younger self watching Oprah, "can't be what they don't see."[^emma-grede-they-re-lying-to-you-about-work-life-EdlXcVu1CTs]
