# Overview

In this rare podcast appearance — part of a 2025 New Year's resolution to speak publicly more — Snapchat co-founder and CEO Evan Spiegel tells Steven Bartlett how he went from a TV-free, book-filled childhood and building his own computer in sixth grade to co-founding Snapchat at 21 while studying product design at Stanford. He describes the Stanford lesson that shaped him most — chase opportunities big enough to reach billions of people, because tech products scale once built — and how an 18-month failed college-application startup, Future Freshmen, taught him and co-founder Bobby Murphy to love what they build and to get customer feedback fast rather than perfecting a product in private.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]

Spiegel recounts Snapchat's origin: a friend's wish for disappearing photos, a prototype (initially called Picaboo) his design class dismissed because screenshots defeated the point, and the screenshot-notification invention that turned the flaw into a feedback loop. Early design choices — opening straight to the camera, tap for photo and hold for video — made it "the fastest way to share a moment," and the app grew purely through friends teaching friends a new behavior: talking with pictures. The company raised $485,000 at a $4.25 million valuation from Lightspeed with roughly 100,000 users, and Spiegel later famously declined Mark Zuckerberg's reported $3 billion acquisition offer at age 23, saying he and Murphy loved the product too much to sell — a decision made easier because each had already sold $10 million of stock, freeing them to "swing for the fences."[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]

The conversation ranges across Snapchat's operating philosophy — a nine-person, totally flat design team where new hires present an idea on day one and "99% of ideas are not good, but 1% is" — the values of kind, smart, creative; being relentlessly copied by Meta; content moderation as a values choice rather than censorship; parenting four boys in the age of AI; and Spiegel's core claim that how much a founder cares is the single biggest predictor of success.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]

# Key points

- Spiegel credits his parents' rules — no TV but any book he wanted — and building his own computer around sixth grade with teaching him that "things that look really complicated on the surface aren't that difficult," an empowering realization that fed his urge to build.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- He says Stanford's business culture taught him to judge ideas by the size of the opportunity: since startup odds are so low, go after something that could reach billions, then use venture capital to scale fast and build the business later.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- His failed startup Future Freshmen taught two lessons: quit when you don't love the product enough to fight through the hard parts, and never spend 18 months building before getting customer feedback — the next venture had a testable prototype within a couple of months.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- Key early Snapchat inventions came from feedback: classmates said disappearing photos were pointless because of screenshots, so he and Murphy built screenshot detection with notifications; users asking for captions got a fast tap-to-type caption bar that became synonymous with the app.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- Snapchat raised $485,000 at a $4.25 million valuation from Lightspeed (whose partner's daughter loved the app) with around 100,000 users; a year of retention data countered investor fears that it was another "flash in the pan" app.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- On the reported $3 billion Facebook offer at 23: Spiegel says it wasn't wisdom but love of the product, plus the fact that he and Murphy had each taken $10 million off the table in an earlier round — he says he never doubted the decision for a moment, and advises founders that secondary sales let you care for your family and still swing for the fences.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- Snapchat's design team is just nine people, totally flat with no titles; new designers must present work on their first day so their "worst fear" of a bad idea happens immediately, unlocking creativity — the operating principle is that the best way to have a good idea is to have lots of ideas, and to "maximize your rate of learning."[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- He cites Safi Bahcall's book Loonshots: enduring innovators pair a large, metrics-driven operating organization with small, flat, unstructured teams of inventors, and success depends on the relationship between the two — mirroring his own design-engineering partnership with Bobby Murphy.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- Snap's values are kind, smart, creative — he distinguishes kind from nice (kindness is telling someone about the food in their teeth, or giving direct feedback that helps them grow) and quotes Murphy's line that "there's no such thing as a brilliant jerk"; he says his biggest scaling regret was embedding values into hiring and performance evaluation too slowly, letting imported Amazon, Google, and Meta cultures dilute Snap's own.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- On Meta's copying (his LinkedIn bio jokes he is "VP of Product at Meta"), Spiegel says the lasting answer is to build things that are hard to copy — evolving from feature to platform, as with Snap's AR ecosystem of Lens Studio, hundreds of thousands of developers, and four million lenses — and he cites independent Dutch and Australian studies finding no negative mental-health effects from Snapchat, unlike Instagram and TikTok.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- He defends content guidelines as First Amendment-protected editorial choice rather than censorship, arguing bans on pornography, violence, and hate are values-based, not political; he characterizes Meta's moderation rollbacks as a "survivalist" strategy of following the political winds.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
- Practices and advice he shares include "council" (a circle-based listening ritual borrowed from his school, used since the decision to stay in LA), Snap's four-plus-day in-office policy to protect creative culture, telling his younger self "everything's going to be okay" because fixing wrong decisions fast matters more than making right ones, and his closing question for entrepreneurs: do you truly love what you're doing, because that love is the fuel.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]

# Takeaways

Spiegel's "formula" is less a formula than a set of convictions the host draws out: care more than anyone else — he calls how much you care the largest predictor of success — generate huge volumes of ideas and get feedback as fast as possible, protect a kind and creative culture with real consequences, and make bold bets you can stay independent to pursue. He describes Snapchat in 2025 as emerging from a "two-year winter into an early spring" after rebuilding its ad platform, says he would never start another tech company because of how brutally it forces personal change ("the hardest thing I've ever had to overcome is myself"), and reframes imposter syndrome as the useful feeling that there is more to learn. These are presented throughout as Spiegel's own views and recollections of Snap's journey.[^exact-formula-used-to-build-a-130-billion-compan-rBM6lGk4-fk]
