# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, host Steven Bartlett interviews Dr. Stacy Sims, an exercise physiologist and nutrition scientist with over 100 peer-reviewed studies, who argues that nearly all sports science protocols — training, fueling, recovery, heat acclimatization, fasting, cold plunges — are built on data from 18-to-22-year-old college-age men and then generalized to women, whose physiology responds very differently.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]

Sims traces her motivation to a university metabolism study that discarded her results as an "anomaly" because her menstrual cycle hormones did not match the male participants' data, and to being told as a teenager she could not become an Army Ranger or Navy SEAL because she was a girl. She explains the physiological sex differences she says matter for exercise — muscle fiber morphology, the Q angle, smaller hearts and lungs, lower hemoglobin, and a more nutrient-sensitive hypothalamus — and walks through how women should adapt fueling and training across the menstrual cycle, perimenopause, and postmenopause.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]

Her central message, as she puts it, is empowerment rather than limitation: the conversation about sex differences "isn't just for women," and the goal is to teach women to use their physiology to their advantage instead of being generalized to male norms.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]

# Key points

- Sims argues sports science defaults to male subjects because recruitment language, limited funding, and male-dominated study design favor 18-22-year-old men, and researchers avoid women because menstrual cycle hormone fluctuations "interfere" with results.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- She says over 60% of previously sporty girls drop out of sport by age 14 because no one teaches them how puberty changes their bodies — widened hips shift the Q angle and center of gravity, making running, jumping, and landing feel unfamiliar.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- On ACL injuries, Sims cites a roughly 3:1 to 4:1 ratio of tears in women versus men, linked to the Q angle and quad dominance; she notes 30 players missed the 2023 Women's World Cup with ACL injuries and says prevention hinges on strengthening the posterior chain (hamstrings, glutes, calves) and training lateral and single-leg landing mechanics.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- She argues women's hypothalamus is far more sensitive to nutrient density: baseline intake to avoid endocrine dysfunction is about 15 calories per kilogram of fat-free mass for men but 30 for women, which is why fasted training and calorie restriction that lean men out can cause women to lose lean mass, store fat, and downregulate thyroid function (within four days of a 20-hour "warrior fast," she claims).[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- Rather than fasted workouts, she advises women to eat a small pre-training dose (around 15g protein before strength work, or 15g protein plus 30g carbohydrate before cardio) and says women's metabolism returns to baseline 30-40 minutes post-exercise versus 2-18 hours for men, so women should get about 35g of protein within 45 minutes of training, while men can wait hours with only 20g.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- Sims contends exercise is a stronger stimulus for autophagy and longevity benefits than fasting, and she is "anti-keto" for both sexes because a 70-80%-fat diet reduces gut microbiome diversity — which matters especially for women since gut bacteria participate in sex hormone recirculation via the liver's "second pass."[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- On cold plunges, she says the popular 0-4°C ice bath findings are male data; for women, roughly 15-16°C (about 55°F) produces the same beneficial response because women vasoconstrict first and ice-cold water becomes too much of a threat. In the sauna, women need longer sessions (20-30 minutes versus her husband's 10) and more days to achieve the same cardiovascular adaptations.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- Her supplement recommendations for women include creatine at 3-5 grams once daily (no loading, unlike bodybuilding's 5g four times a day), which she says supports gut integrity, brain metabolism, and mood — citing randomized trials where women taking creatine emerged from depressive episodes better than with an SSRI alone — plus vitamin D3, omega-3s from the late 30s onward, and every-other-day high-bioavailability iron for low ferritin, timed around the hepcidin response to training.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- Across the menstrual cycle, she advises pushing high-intensity work and PRs in the low-hormone follicular phase (roughly days 6-13), adding more carbohydrate around high-intensity work after ovulation plus about 12% more protein, and deloading in the four to five days before the bleed; she recommends tracking sessional rating of perceived exertion and the bleed pattern itself as an early-warning sign of low energy availability, which she says affects up to 55% of recreational female athletes.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- On perimenopause (starting around age 35, with average menopause at 52), she describes rising LDL and A1C, visceral fat gain, and sleep disruption as estrogen and progesterone ratios shift, and argues women can no longer rely on hormones for adaptation — so they need true high-intensity sprint work, plyometrics, and power-based resistance training three times a week with less volume and more quality, plus higher protein.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- She deliberately says "menopause hormone therapy" rather than "hormone replacement therapy," arguing the goal is low physiologic dosing to attenuate symptom-driving change, not to replace reproductive-year hormone levels or serve as anti-aging; she also states there is no evidence hormone therapy prevents dementia.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
- On GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic, she calls them a "brilliant tool" for people with severe obesity but warns the rapid weight loss is substantially lean mass, and says no one teaches users the strength training and nutrition behavior changes needed to come off the drugs safely.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]

# Takeaways

The guest's core argument is that women are not small men: from the hypothalamus to the gut microbiome to ligament mechanics, female physiology responds differently to food, stress, heat, cold, and training, yet the prevailing protocols were never designed for it. Sims urges women to eat around training rather than fast, lift heavy and jump as hormones decline, track their own cycles as a built-in stress gauge, and treat popular trends — keto, ice baths, warrior fasting, creatine loading — with the skepticism due to advice derived from male data. She frames the episode as a conversation for men too, since better understanding of female physiology improves research, relationships, and health for everyone.[^exercise-nutrition-scientist-the-truth-about-exe-It5_C6AF1pk]
