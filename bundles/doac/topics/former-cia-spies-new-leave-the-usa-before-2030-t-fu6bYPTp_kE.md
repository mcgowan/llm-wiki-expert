# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Andrew and Jihi Bustamante, a married former CIA "tandem couple" — he a field/case officer and mission planner, she a targeter who profiled individuals for capture or kill during the war on terror — telling for the first time the story behind their book Shadow Cell. The guests recount being pulled into a counterintelligence operation after a foreign ally warned the CIA that a mole inside "Falcon House" (the CIA group focused on a code-named adversary country, "Falcon") was passing operations, officers, and assets to that enemy. They say the CIA initially approved their manuscript in 2019, reclassified everything in 2022 amid shifting geopolitics, and backed down only when the couple threatened a First Amendment lawsuit.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]

The couple describe building a "shadow cell" in a friendly neighboring country ("Wolf") — an espionage team deliberately modeled on terrorist cell structures and compartmentalization, using "sticks and bricks" foundational tradecraft rather than technology — to create new intelligence sources the mole could not access, tempting the mole to overreach and make a legally prosecutable mistake. Andrew recounts being exposed in Falcon, likely by the mole: detecting a multi-vehicle surveillance team on a surveillance detection route, accidentally locking eyes with a watcher in an arcade, sending Jihi the coded message "I'm coming home early," and bluffing his way through an airport interrogation to escape. His alias was burned and he could never return.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]

Beyond the operation, the conversation ranges over CIA tradecraft (dry-cleaning routes, dead drops, passport swaps, draft-folder communications learned from terrorists), the CIA's front companies and "black budget," digital privacy, the Epstein and Snowden cases, and the couple's dark assessment of America's trajectory — the reason they say they intend to leave the United States.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]

# Key points

- The guests say the CIA only learned it had a mole because a foreign ally (a Five Eyes-type partner) reported it; because an ally's word is legally just a foreigner's claim, the CIA had to engineer situations in which the mole would make mistakes on US soil to build a prosecutable case.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- Their operation was deliberately compartmentalized: headquarters told them to build new intelligence sources in Falcon, coordinate only with local leadership in Wolf, and "don't tell us" — starving the mole of access so any probing would expose him.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- Andrew describes previously undisclosed tradecraft: "dry cleaning" (cleansing routes through a neutral third country with passport swaps so the hostile country traces you to the wrong origin), dead drops, coded nicknames like "bomber jacket" for memorizing surveillance profiles, and three-phase surveillance detection routes (suspect, confirm, collect).[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- The shadow cell copied terrorist methods on purpose: Andrew argues the US was the only country that fought the global war on terror — spending $8 trillion only to hand Afghanistan back — so lessons learned from Al-Qaeda's cell structure and low-tech methods were a competitive advantage no adversary shared, including the couple's own draft-email "sign of life" communication plan.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- Andrew was built into the cell as the exposed "node" and tripwire; when Falcon's surveillance team "starburst" in an arcade and a watcher locked eyes with him for about two and a half seconds, he knew he was burned — the CIA and the cell later concluded no operational error was made and only the mole's leak could explain his exposure.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- He escaped by acting boring rather than fleeing dramatically: he flew out on a normal itinerary, endured a secondary interrogation at the airport using mirroring and counter-elicitation techniques (including deliberate silence), and was released when the two Falcon officers gave up.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- The guests say the operation succeeded: it built new sources, preserved US intelligence advantage while legacy sources were compromised, seeded the case file that led the FBI to lure the mole back to US soil and arrest him (they say around 2018-2019), and its cell model reportedly influenced John Brennan's 2014 CIA reorganization. Bartlett's research team names Jerry Chun Shing Lee as the likely mole; the couple are legally obliged to neither confirm nor deny.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- They describe the mole as a witting agent paid hundreds of thousands of dollars, and argue moles are recruited less with money than by exploiting the CIA's own culture of withheld validation — a foreign service fills the void by telling an officer "your own service doesn't understand how valuable you are."[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- On privacy, the guests claim no phone or device is truly secure, border agents can clone travelers' drives, most major social networks have been approached by intelligence agencies and "the vast majority of them cooperate," and FISA warrants can open Google and Apple accounts — though they insist the government has "zero interest in the average person."[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- Andrew's read on Jeffrey Epstein is that he fits the profile of an independent intelligence asset collecting on US citizens for possibly several countries, and that the most probable explanation of his death is a paid hit inside the jail arranged by someone with compromising exposure — while noting the CIA runs both fake and genuinely profitable real front companies funded through the "black budget."[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- On America's future, Andrew gives a 60/40 split: a 60% chance Americans won't like where the current transition ends, followed by 15-25 years of repairing the economy, alliances, and trust in government — citing an ever-stronger executive, policy gridlock, fear-driven nationalism ("nationalism is not a product of pride, it's a product of fear," quoting Ursula K. Le Guin), and infighting that only benefits adversaries.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
- Jihi, born in Venezuela, watched a thriving democracy collapse within about five years of her birth; she argues "that can't happen here" is always a lie, which is why the couple rent rather than buy, stay mobile, and have been planning to leave the US — Andrew has said before 2026, and the episode's framing warns viewers to consider leaving before 2030.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]

# Takeaways

The guests present espionage as a team sport of foundational discipline rather than James Bond heroics, and the CIA as a morally ambivalent institution — willing to knowingly dangle its own officers as bait, trade in anything that wins a target's trust, and cultivate loyalty through withheld validation. Their life advice mirrors their operational worldview: assume nothing is private, never assume collapse "can't happen here," read the writing on the wall, stay mobile — and, because you never know when everything changes, seize joy with family and in the present rather than deferring it to retirement. They close by framing America as irreversibly transitioning into something new: participate in building it, or decide where else you will go.[^former-cia-spies-new-leave-the-usa-before-2030-t-fu6bYPTp_kE]
