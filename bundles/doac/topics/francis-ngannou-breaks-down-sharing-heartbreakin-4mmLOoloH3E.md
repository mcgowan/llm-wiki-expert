# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett interviews Francis Ngannou, the former UFC heavyweight champion who grew up in a village in West Cameroon and reached the top of professional fighting by way of the Sahara, the forests of northern Morocco and a parking lot in Paris.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] Bartlett says he first heard the story in a hotel room in Paris and could not believe it was true, and that in three or four hundred interviews he has never encountered anything like it.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]

Ngannou tells the story with almost no self-pity: the mud-walled kitchen he was photographed sitting in with his mother and his UFC belt, reading schoolbooks by the cooking fire, the sand quarry he started working in at nine, and the day at thirteen when he was thrown out of class over unpaid school fees and decided he would become a professional fighter.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] His account of the migration route — drinking from a well fouled with dead animals, skeletons in the desert, barbed-wire scars, six failed crossings in an inflatable dinghy he could not swim beside — is delivered as a sequence of practical problems, with the recurring explanation that when a dream is big enough it blinds you to everything in front of you.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]

The second half of the conversation turns to what he gave up to leave the UFC, his contested loss to Anthony Joshua, and then to the death of his 15-month-old son Kobe a few months before the interview.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] Ngannou, who had built his entire life around never being powerless in front of a sick family member the way he was when his father died, says the one person he should have been able to fight for was someone he never got a chance to help — and that he is now trying to make the loss a reason to keep going rather than a reason to stop.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]

# Key points

- Ngannou was born in West Cameroon in 1986; Bartlett cites an average income there of about $1,500 a year at the time, and Ngannou replies that this "is a lot of money" — his family lived on less than $1,000 — in a village of roughly 11,000 people, in a house whose walls and floor were mud.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He says the childhood was not happy but that he owes who he is to it: no shoes, trousers full of holes, no pens or notebooks, nothing to eat at break time, and reading by the light of the cooking fire because there was no paraffin for the lamp.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He began work in a sand mine at nine, shovelling sand, and says that gave him his answer to the humiliation of comparison — the other children had more, but they had not earned it, and he had earned his little, so he was not beneath them.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- The turning point came at thirteen, when he was expelled from the classroom over unpaid annual school fees; that day he decided to become a professional boxer, despite there being no gym within roughly 50 miles and never having seen one.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He describes his violent father as "the example for me of what not to do", says relatives predicted he would turn out the same, and explains that he chose regulated combat sport precisely because it had rules — win a fight there and you are celebrated rather than blamed.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- His father died when Ngannou was fifteen, after months bedridden at home with a rotting leg because the family could not afford a hospital; Ngannou says the nightmares of him screaming in pain, and later the question of what he would do if his mother got sick, are what pushed him to act.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He left school at seventeen, left the village at twenty-two for Douala to find his first boxing gym, and left Cameroon at about twenty-six in 2012 — travelling by bus, train and smugglers' cars through Nigeria, Niger, Algeria and into Morocco, crossing the Sahara in the back of a pickup with 25 people in roughly 20-22 hours, past skeletons he learned to look away from.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- In southern Algeria he drank from a nearly dry well full of dead animals and leaves, reasoning that the choice was to "die now or die a little later" — a decision he says is easy when those are the only two options.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He spent almost a year in Morocco, living in the Gurugu forest and scavenging rotten produce and chicken legs from market bins at night; the fence into Melilla ran 12-14 miles with up to three lines of barbed wire around 7 metres high and guard posts every 20-30 metres, so scouting parties spent days mapping shift changes and prayer times before an attempt.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- Ngannou has barbed-wire scars on his fingers, stomach and feet from the one attempt where he reached the fence, and describes falling into wire laid in a trench because people pushed forward from behind; he pulled back, deciding he would "rather be alive without being in Europe than dead in Europe".[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He failed six sea crossings in an inflatable dinghy of the kind sold for swimming pools, becoming the group's captain despite not being able to swim; anyone caught, he says, was driven to the Algerian border and dumped in the desert under an arrangement he believes was financed by the European Union.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- The seventh attempt succeeded on 2 April 2013, a date he picked himself by checking wind speed and direction daily; he made the crossing feverish and unable to tell anyone he was ill because he was the captain, found a table-sized patch of clear rock to inflate the boat, and after roughly an hour and a half of paddling called the Red Cross using a helicopter overhead as his only landmark — throwing away every identifying document only once he was certain of rescue.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He spent about 53 days in a Spanish detention centre, said the loss of freedom was harder than sleeping rough, and arrived in Paris on 9 June 2013 at twenty-six, sleeping in a parking lot for around two months while walking into a boxing gym the day after he arrived and asking simply for somewhere to train because he wanted to be a world champion.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- The man who took his number that day spoke to the head coach, got him free training, gave him shoes and later the keys to an empty apartment for two months, and told him he did not need to tell his story to everybody; Ngannou says he had no expectations of him at all, which is why the help meant so much.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- Because his papers made a boxing career in France impractical, he was pointed towards MMA — a sport he had never heard of — joining the MMA Factory under Fernand Lopez in 2013 and having his first fight within four months; after only five fights in Europe, during which his coach posted on Facebook asking whether anyone on the continent would fight him, his manager Thiago Okamura pitched him to the UFC.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- His UFC debut was in Orlando in 2015 for $10,000 plus a $10,000 win bonus, about half of which survived commissions; the moment that mattered, he says, was being met at the airport with his name on a tablet and calling his mother to say he had made it — he had entered Europe through the service door and America on the red carpet.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- Bartlett notes Ngannou holds the hardest punch ever recorded by the UFC, equivalent to 93 horsepower against a smart car's 80; Ngannou held the heavyweight title from 2021 to 2023, and says the 2018 title fight he lost was the most valuable of his career because he had never been an athlete and had no idea how to prepare.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He left the UFC in January 2023 over a contract he describes as entirely one-sided: exclusivity forever, no guarantee of fights, no healthcare obligation, and the promoter's right to extend his deal by six months if he turned down a bout for any reason — including one they knew he could not take.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- He says he was "broke" by heavyweight-champion standards and living off loans, never earned a million dollars for a fight and topped out around £500,000, and turned down a lucrative renewal knowing he might end up farming in Africa instead: "freedom is not free, you have to give something in order to get that."[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- On Dana White's claim that he wanted lesser opponents for more money, Ngannou answers that taking Tyson Fury as a first professional boxing match hardly sounds like an easy option, and that White simply had to say something to get out of an embarrassing situation.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]

- On the death of his son Kobe at fifteen months, Ngannou says he cannot explain how a person can occupy that much space in so little time, that the purpose of his fighting collapsed because the only person he could truly fight for was someone he never got a chance to help — the boy was not sick, there was no hospital to take him to — and that it made him feel like a failure.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
- Asked about grief and therapy, he says nobody teaches you the process and questions what a therapist could take away or bring back, comparing it to learning to live with a handicap; he admits to being tired of being tough, to doubting his own capability, and to discovering he is more emotional than he thought.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]

# Takeaways

Ngannou's own reading of his story is deliberately unromantic: he says he did not know boxing would work, that everyone including his mother thought he had lost his mind when he sold what he had to chase it, and that people only call it genius in hindsight — he simply refused to give up and kept trying.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] From that he draws his closing argument, that the real failure is not trying, and that most limits are ones people set for themselves while debating what is possible instead of acting.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] He also rejects the idea of a summit: every peak reveals a higher mountain, and what he says grounds him is going home and standing in the places he stood fifteen years ago to feel the distance travelled.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E] Asked when he last realised he had it all wrong, he answers that it was when his son died — and the chapter he describes ahead is an attempt to turn that into a new reason to fight rather than a reason to stop.[^francis-ngannou-breaks-down-sharing-heartbreakin-4mmLOoloH3E]
