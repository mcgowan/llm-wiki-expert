# Overview

In this Diary Of A CEO episode, conservationist and author Paul — the "Anaconda guy" behind the organization Jungle Keepers — describes the roughly 20 years he has spent living out of a backpack in the Peruvian Amazon, learning jungle survival from indigenous mentor JJ of the Ese'eja people, and building a movement that has turned loggers and gold miners into conservation rangers protecting a river basin of ancient rainforest.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g] The centerpiece of the conversation is what the guest presents as world-first footage of a first-contact encounter with the Mashco Piro, a nomadic uncontacted tribe so isolated he says they have "never heard of a spoon or the wheel or Jesus" — an encounter reached by compressing a two-day boat journey into one night through a violent thunderstorm, navigating by the eye-shine of caimans.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]

The guest describes the encounter as fear on both sides: naked warriors with seven-foot bows emerging across a river, demanding plantains and rope, asking "how do we tell the bad guys from the good guys," and warning outsiders to stop cutting down the trees they call their gods.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g] He recounts the violence surrounding these tribes — a ranger shot in the head by an arrow years earlier, his friend George shot through the lung the day after the encounter, a friend's father killed in a stream ambush, and a mass grave of a related clan attributed to narco-traffickers — arguing their aggression is a defensive response to being hunted by loggers, miners, and traffickers and boxed in by deforestation.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]

Around this story the episode weaves the guest's broader arc: the Discovery Channel "Eaten Alive" debacle that he says set his career back a decade, Jane Goodall's endorsement that launched him as an author, his near-quitting moment during COVID followed a week later by billionaire funder Dax Dasilva's five-year commitment, and his closing argument that this is the last generation with a chance to save the Amazon's ecosystems.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]

# Key points

- The guest describes the Amazon as larger than the lower 48 US states, holding roughly one-fifth of Earth's fresh water and producing about a fifth of its oxygen, with a 150–160 ft canopy holding half the rainforest's life — in his words the most biodiverse terrestrial biome in the fossil record.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- He credits his training to JJ, an indigenous man who had no shoes until age 13 and can track jaguars, read a beach "like last night's newspaper," and use his own foot callus as fish bait — and whose challenge after loggers burned their forest ("Do you see anybody? You have to do something") launched the mission that became Jungle Keepers.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- Jungle Keepers, directed by the guest and JJ, protects around 130,000 acres (elsewhere he cites a 300,000-acre goal for the watershed), employs former loggers and gold miners as paid conservation rangers, and is working toward creating a national park covering the entire river basin.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- The Mashco Piro ("wild Piro people") kept the river wild for centuries as "the original jungle keepers"; their existence was so contested that a Peruvian president once called them a myth invented to scare loggers, with seven-foot bamboo-tipped arrows the only physical proof before the encounter.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- During the filmed contact the tribesmen greeted the group with "nomole" ("brothers"), demanded bananas and rope, feared the cameras were guns, and asked how to distinguish good outsiders from the ones who "shooted us with the fire sticks" — while the tribe's women, hidden during the distraction, raided the community's farm of yuca, plantains, and sugarcane.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- An accompanying anthropologist described the tribe as not even Stone Age but "bamboo age" hunter-gatherers — a thousand-year time capsule; they eat mainly turtles and monkeys, do not fish, are not cannibals (the guest debunks AI-faked audio of him claiming otherwise), and communicate in the forest by imitating capuchin monkey and tinamou bird calls to encircle intruders undetected.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- The guest says the footage was withheld and then released deliberately: rapid contact destroys such tribes — outside pathogens like the common cold could wipe them out, as could romanticizing "the last free people" — so the only ethical protection is preserving the forest they live in, since "they can't come on a podcast or address the United Nations."[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- The day after the peaceful contact, the tribe opened fire on a boat driven by the guest's friend George, who took an arrow through the scapula that exited near his belly button and collapsed a lung; he survived after helicopter evacuation, and the guest notes there is effectively no law enforcement in the deep jungle — "the police have no jurisdiction outside of the city."[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- He retells the Discovery Channel "Eaten Alive" episode: promised a science-led show called Expedition Amazon featuring his team's capture of Eleanor, an 18 ft 6 in, 100+ kg anaconda he calls the largest verifiably measured, the network cut the science, renamed the show, and the backlash from the public, PETA, and scientists set him back about 10 years.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- Jane Goodall read chapters he handed her in a manila envelope at a New York talk and endorsed his first book within 48 hours — he says "Jane Goodall saved my life" — and after he privately quit during COVID, funder Dax Dasilva called a week later with a five-year commitment that protected another 100,000 acres and made the organization viable.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- He describes indigenous plant medicine curing in one night a rare tularemia infection that two months of double antibiotics could not, an accidental ayahuasca mega-dose (the elderly shaman fell asleep while boiling the brew) that felt like dying and dissolving into the universe, and cautions he "wouldn't recommend it to anybody."[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
- Against AI-era doom, the guest is contrarian and optimistic: he cites tigers recovering from ~3,000 toward 5–6,000, humpback whales rebounding from ~1,000 to near pre-whaling numbers, and the healed ozone hole as proof that "seemingly terminal" situations can be turned around, urging listeners to "go touch some grass" rather than doom-scroll.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]

# Takeaways

The guest's core argument is that the fates of the Mashco Piro, the Amazon's wildlife, and humanity's own life-support systems are one and the same problem, solvable by protecting land through the indigenous-led ranger model of Jungle Keepers, funded directly by ordinary donors "for the price of a Starbucks coffee once a month."[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g] Alongside the conservation case he offers a life philosophy of relentlessness — "the master has failed more times than the beginner has even tried" — while warning against survivorship bias in taking advice from those, like himself, who bet everything and happened to make it; host Steven Bartlett signs up as a monthly donor on air.[^i-met-an-uncontacted-tribe-they-killed-my-friend-sR7S2Q3c04g]
