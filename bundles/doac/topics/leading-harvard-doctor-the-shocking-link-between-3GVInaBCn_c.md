# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Chris Palmer, a Harvard psychiatrist of some 28 years and author of *Brain Energy*, who came to the field furious with it: his mother suffered a breakdown that escalated from major depression to chronic psychosis when he was about 12, lost custody of her eight children and all her money, and he himself left home before finishing high school with chronic depression, OCD and suicidality, later becoming homeless while living with her.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c] He dedicates the book to her — "my futile attempts to save you from the ravages of mental illness lit a fire in me that burns to this day" — and says his mission is the hundreds of millions of people whose lives are being devastated the same way.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]

Palmer's central claim is that chronic serious mental disorders — depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, addiction, eating disorders, autism, Alzheimer's — are metabolic disorders affecting the brain, and that the unifying mechanism is mitochondrial dysfunction.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c] On this view neurotransmitter imbalance, hormone imbalance, inflammation, gut microbiome effects and unresolved trauma are not competing explanations but downstream consequences that mitochondria connect: when cells cannot properly convert food and oxygen into energy, brain cells malfunction, and that malfunction is what we observe as symptoms.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]

He is careful to say he does not want to remove anyone's access to medication or psychotherapy — "if treatment is working for someone... fantastic, you're lucky, keep doing it" — but insists the field must confront how many patients do everything asked of them and still do not recover, to the point that governments are now treating some mental illnesses as terminal.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c] His hopeful counter-message is that damaged cells can be repaired, and he offers his own metabolic-syndrome reversal and the case of a patient named Doris as evidence that recovery, not lifelong management, can be the goal.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]

# Key points

- Palmer says mental disorders are now the leading cause of disease burden and disability worldwide; pre-pandemic roughly 1 billion people — about 13% of the world's population — had a mental or substance use disorder in a single year (2017), and rates are higher since.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- He cites 20-year US trends: autism rates quadrupled, ADHD rates "through the roof," adult bipolar diagnoses doubled with child and adolescent rates rising exponentially, and Gallup's 2023 survey recording all-time highs in both current and lifetime depression prevalence.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- On outcomes, he points to the largest depression trial ever run (over 4,000 patients at leading US academic centres): only about 30% achieved remission on the first antidepressant; the published figure after four treatment levels was 67% remission, but half the participants dropped out and critics who hold the study to its pre-registered remission criteria put the figure nearer 35%.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- For schizophrenia he cites a study of 6,000 patients in which only 4% achieved recovery — full symptom remission plus decent quality of life and the ability to work or study — statistics he says are little better than 50 years ago.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- He argues newer drugs are no better because they are built on the same mechanisms, and treats the assisted-dying developments as proof the system knows it is failing: Canada was set to permit assisted suicide for treatment-resistant mental illness in March 2024, and the UK has begun labelling some eating disorders "terminal."[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- Against the "we're just talking about it more" rebuttal, he says ask teachers of 30 years whether they were previously failing to notice tantrums, self-injury and despair in classrooms, and points to emergency-room crowding and to deaths of despair — suicides plus alcohol and overdose deaths — doubling in 20 years while the US suicide rate rose about 30%: "morticians know how to recognize" death.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- On genetics, he says there is no bipolar gene; risk genes raise risk across schizophrenia, epilepsy, autism and depression simultaneously, and their common pathway is metabolism and mitochondria — he cites research on a rare high-risk schizophrenia gene concluding it acts through mitochondria, and notes epigenetic changes are heritable too.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- Mitochondria, he explains, are far more than "the powerhouse of the cell": hundreds or thousands per cell, descended from an engulfed bacterium in an event thought to have happened once on Earth, they move, fuse, divide and cluster around the nucleus influencing which genes are expressed; roughly 90% of metabolism occurs in them.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- His trauma model: the acute fight-or-flight response is normal, not disorder — cortisol, adrenaline, raised glucose, inflammation and powerful memory encoding all serve survival. Pathology begins when the response fails to switch off, producing hypermetabolism and diverting resources away from cellular maintenance, with repair instructions sequestered in stress granules until cells fall into disrepair and brain cells begin to malfunction — the point at which he would call it a mental illness.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- Palmer estimates that 95% of mental health clinicians find the idea that diet matters laughable, while he says only about 7% of US citizens show no marker of metabolic syndrome, leaving roughly 93% with pre-diabetes, abnormal lipids, high blood pressure or excess abdominal fat.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- The associations Bartlett reads back from *Brain Energy*: obesity carries a 50% higher risk of bipolar disorder and 25% higher risk of anxiety or depression; weight gain around puberty is linked to a 400% increase in depression by 24; insulin resistance at age nine is associated with a 500% increase in psychosis-risk mental state; and every mental disorder raises Alzheimer's risk, from 50% to as much as 2,000%.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- On autism he says the mitochondrial theory dates to 1985, and that a fourfold rise in 20 years cannot be genetic: maternal obesity doubles the risk of an autistic child, maternal diabetes doubles it, both together quadruple it, and paternal obesity doubles it — a point he stresses is "not about fat shaming" but about parents' metabolic dysfunction, potentially driven by ultra-processed food, pesticides, microplastics and forever chemicals, being transmitted to children. He expects COVID to worsen neurodevelopmental statistics further.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- Palmer recounts his own reversal — developing high blood pressure, high cholesterol and pre-diabetes while lean and following the low-fat, largely processed diet then recommended, then clearing his metabolic syndrome within three months of going low-carbohydrate and finding his mental health "better than it had ever been in my entire life."[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- His flagship case is Doris, abused in childhood, diagnosed with schizophrenia at 17, unresponsive to decades of antipsychotics and mood stabilisers, 330 pounds and with at least six suicide attempts between ages 68 and 70; referred to a Duke weight-loss clinic using the ketogenic diet at 70, she saw hallucinations and delusions drop within two weeks, reached full remission within months, came off all psychiatric medication within about six months, lost 150 pounds and lived symptom-free until dying of pneumonia at 85.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- He notes the ketogenic diet was developed over a century ago purely to stop seizures and remains an evidence-based epilepsy treatment — about a third of treatment-resistant patients become seizure-free and another third improve substantially — usually run for two to five years rather than for life, suggesting it repairs rather than merely suppresses; fasting works through the same mitochondrial mechanisms but is unsafe for underweight people, including those with eating disorders, severe depression-related weight loss or cancer.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
- On stimulants and depressants he uses an accelerator-and-brake analogy: caffeine blocks adenosine receptors and can help a sluggish brain, but overdoing caffeine, alcohol or marijuana pushes accelerator and brake at once and generates oxidative stress — a marker he says unites the metabolic and mental disorders. He declines a one-size-fits-all diet prescription and, for children showing early signs, recommends sleep, less screen time, human contact and purpose, and trying non-pharmacological sleep routines before reaching for pills including melatonin.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]

# Takeaways

Palmer's argument is that psychiatry has been treating symptoms of a metabolic problem it never diagnosed, and that reframing mental illness as mitochondrial dysfunction in the brain both explains why prevalence tracks obesity and diabetes and opens interventions — diet, fasting, sleep, substance use, medication where it helps — that the field currently dismisses.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c] He hedges with a scientist's "might" on population-level causation while saying he knows with certainty that metabolic treatment has healed people with chronic, debilitating illness.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c] Asked what he would like to be known for as a person rather than for his work, he answers that he was someone who had given up on himself and believed he would be dead before 20 — and that if it changed for him, it can change for others.[^leading-harvard-doctor-the-shocking-link-between-3GVInaBCn_c]
