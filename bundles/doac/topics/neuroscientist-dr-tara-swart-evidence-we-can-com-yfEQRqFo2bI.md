# Overview

Dr. Tara Swart — neuroscientist, medical doctor, and former psychiatrist, and previously the show's most-viewed guest at over 20 million views — returns to reveal a secret she withheld from her first appearance: her husband Robin died of leukemia in 2021, two days before their fourth wedding anniversary. She describes being "totally lost and broken," then noticing an unprecedented number of robins in her garden, being woken six weeks after his death by a thump to her shoulder and seeing a hazy apparition of him beside her bed, and — after finding mediums unimpressive — deciding that if communication with the dead is possible, a brain-optimization expert should learn to do it herself. She says she is now "100%" sure she communicates with him daily, mostly through requested signs, and has written a book, "The Signs," about the journey.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]

Swart frames her case as a scientist's, not a mystic's: she acknowledges she spent four years privately auditing herself against psychiatric criteria — she experienced thought insertion, a symptom of schizophrenia, and argues grief itself is neurochemically "like psychosis." Her supporting evidence spans a claimed literature review finding humans have 34 senses rather than five, the shared-trait-vulnerability model linking creativity and mental illness through hyperconnectivity, novelty salience, and low latent inhibition, terminal lucidity cases in which irreversibly damaged brains become briefly coherent before death, and near-death-experience research including Dr. Bruce Greyson's 5,000+ recorded cases and the "red MG" story, in which an ICU patient learned of his nurse's death from the nurse herself during an NDE. Her conclusion: the mind "cannot be solely emerging from physical matter."[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]

Bartlett explicitly plays devil's advocate, pressing her on confirmation bias, the reticular activating system, probability ("if coincidence didn't happen, that would be a miracle"), and whether altered states are just neurochemistry; Swart concedes several points — she cannot prove her claims, saying instead "you can't prove that this isn't true" — and rests her practical case on the documented benefits of believing in something transcendent. The conversation closes with her raw account of Robin's final weeks and her view that noticing beauty, nature, creativity, and community are trainable routes to receiving signs.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]

# Key points

- The guest claims a literature review shows humans have 34 senses as currently understood, not five — many non-conscious, like sensing blood pH and oxygen/CO2 balance — and argues the brain filters down the mind's capability so we can function on the material plane.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- She kept her husband's death secret for four years partly because of professional taboo: as a psychiatrist she had "been part of teams that locked people up" for describing experiences "not that dissimilar" to her own.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Her body "remembered" trauma her mind did not: from October 4th — the anniversary of taking Robin home to die, a date she had consciously forgotten — she suffered six to seven weeks of unexplained full-body pain, which she resolved through somatic work; she cites the serotonin hypothesis and fascia research as emerging mechanisms for how trauma is stored in tissue beyond the reach of talking therapy.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Her signature sign story: burnt out on the Navajo Nation before the second anniversary of Robin's death, she asked him for the sign of a phoenix — then passed a "Phoenix Garden" restaurant daily in Oklahoma City, and her unplanned flight out on the anniversary itself was routed through Phoenix, Arizona.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Challenged on confirmation bias, she doesn't deny it — "I say use it to your advantage" — and describes tightening criteria (a specific symbol, seen three times, by a deadline) to make signs harder to dismiss.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- On terminal lucidity, she cites a 2009 case of a nonverbal 82-year-old Alzheimer's patient who became fully lucid the day before dying, and quotes Professor Alexander Batthyany's suggestion that at the border of life and death we glimpse that "the mind and body can operate independently of each other."[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Her NDE evidence includes Dr. Mary Neal (submerged 15-20 minutes), Dr. Eben Alexander ("Proof of Heaven"), and Dr. Bruce Greyson's 50 years of research with over 5,000 personal cases and 10,000+ globally — most strikingly the red MG story, where a patient's NDE contained information (his nurse's fatal crash in a red MG) he could not have known.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- She invokes David Eagleman's brain-as-radio-receiver idea and Donald Hoffman's proposal that consciousness, not space-time, is fundamental — stressing that neither can be proven false and that science requires challenging the status quo.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Dark retreats — 7 to 49 days in complete darkness, from Tibetan practice — are presented as a way to emulate near-death experiences: after days of melatonin-driven sleep, practitioners hallucinate lights, then animals, then deities, and emerge with joy for life, less fear of death, and more compassion; she also cites a paper finding certain conscious connected breathwork matches a moderate psilocybin dose.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- She argues merely learning about NDEs confers lasting benefits — research on students shows increased compassion, gratitude, and reduced materialism persisting over a year — and that believing in something transcendent is healthy, though she accepts Bartlett's counterpoint that transcendent belief can also fuel destructive dogma.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- Her grief advice is tiered: first, feel the emotions and use talking therapy or friends; second, add physical/somatic therapy (dance, drumming, massage, yoga — practices she notes ancient cultures used, like Greek mourners beating their chests), nature, and creative outlets; third, if inclined, be open to signs.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
- On human sensory potential she cites Joy Milne, the nurse who smelled her husband's Parkinson's years before diagnosis (leading to a new swab test), blind people repurposing visual cortex for echolocation, and Russell Foster's once-ridiculed, now-vindicated melanopsin cells in the retina.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]

# Takeaways

The guest's argument is less a proof than an invitation: she asks listeners to "just try it" — request a sign, notice beauty, tend the gut-brain axis, spend time in nature — on the grounds that the practices are beneficial regardless and that science has repeatedly validated claims once ridiculed. The host remains explicitly skeptical but lands on open-minded agnosticism, framing the episode as a case study in holding rigor and possibility together. Swart, for her part, says her remaining work is to stop letting guilt dim her — to "let my light really shine" — and that Robin's unconditional love, the answer she gives to the show's closing question, is what convinced her such things exist at all.[^neuroscientist-dr-tara-swart-evidence-we-can-com-yfEQRqFo2bI]
