# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett interviews JL Collins, the 75-year-old author of The Simple Path to Wealth — a book that grew out of a blog he wrote to teach his daughter about money and has sold over a million copies. Collins distills his philosophy into one sentence: avoid debt, live on less than you earn, and invest the surplus. He reframes money not just as a means of exchange but as a tool for "buying your freedom," arguing that dependence on a paycheck is "a form of slavery" and that "F-you money" — the growing cushion accumulated on the way to full financial independence — buys leverage and options long before work becomes optional.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]

The episode's most provocative claim gives it its title: Collins argues that people seeking financial independence at a young age probably should not buy a house. In his telling, the real estate and banking industries push buyers to stretch to the largest mortgage a bank will approve, houses inflate cost of living through renovations, furniture, taxes, and unpredictable variable expenses (a $20,000 roof, a $25,000 septic system), and home ownership destroys the career flexibility that young people need most. He stresses he is not anti-house — he has owned houses most of his adult life — but calls them "an expensive indulgence," not an investment.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]

On investing, Collins calls stocks "the single most effective, strongest wealth-building tool that's ever been created" but only over long horizons, recommending broad-based, low-cost index funds like Vanguard's VTSAX total stock market fund and warning against panic-selling, market timing, tinkering, Bitcoin speculation, tech-sector concentration, and percentage-of-assets financial advisers. Bartlett contributes his own history of spending for self-esteem, debt avoidance, and a cautionary discussion of divorce as a wealth destroyer.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]

# Key points

- Collins's "simple path" in one sentence: avoid debt, live on less than you earn, invest the surplus — because "you can never be financially independent if you're carrying around debt," which he likens to a swimmer competing with a weight strapped on.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- He argues buying a house makes early financial independence harder: buyers typically stretch to the maximum a bank will lend ("the bank wants you to do that — that's how they make the most money"), the mortgage is "just the starting point" before renovations, furniture, taxes, and maintenance, and unlike fixed rent, housing costs are unpredictable variable expenses.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- Houses also destroy flexibility: transaction costs of buying and selling are enormous, a mortgage psychologically anchors you against career moves, and an unsellable house makes you an "accidental landlord" — while renting lets you leave for an opportunity with only a security deposit at stake.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- Bartlett cites supporting data: US home prices are up over 300% since 1980, outpacing inflation and wages; mortgage rates surged past 7% in 2023; median wages rose only about 15% since 2000 while home prices more than doubled.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- Collins calls the "tyranny of the must-haves" the main obstacle to wealth: the more must-haves in your life, the less likely you are to become financially independent; he saved 50% of his income from his first $10,000-a-year job and insists it was never deprivation because freedom was the thing he wanted most.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- He argues a large income can be an impediment: a friend making $1 million a year (with an $800,000 bonus) was broke from lifestyle competition, while a high-school friend who never earned more than $40,000 became financially independent by following the principles.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- On debt payoff, his advice is the "avalanche" approach: pay minimums on everything except the highest-interest debt, attack that first, and note that the discipline built while clearing debt becomes the cash-flow engine for wealth-building afterward.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- His core investment recommendation is broad-based, low-cost index funds — specifically VTSAX, which holds roughly 3,600 US public companies, is cap-weighted, and "self-cleanses" as failing companies (his example: Sears) fade and their replacements (Walmart, Amazon) automatically rise into the index.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- He describes the 4% guideline (Bill Bengen, validated by the Trinity study): multiply annual spending by 25 to find your financial-independence number — $100,000 a year of spending requires $2.5 million invested — and recounts that even a banker with $5 million couldn't believe the math because compounding "hockey-sticks" beyond intuition.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- Stocks are volatile short-term but "stunningly reliable" over 10-20 year horizons; success is "100% dependent on tying yourself to the mast during the storm" — Bartlett shares that he panic-sold his first £10,000 Facebook investment, and Collins cites Vanguard/Berkshire data that men trade 45% more often than women and underperform because of tinkering and overtrading.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- Collins declines to own Bitcoin, calling it a speculation rather than an investment ("too volatile to serve as currency"), and rejects tech-heavy bets like the Nasdaq 100 despite its ~20% annual decade-long run, because sector dominance rotates and the total-market index captures whatever wins next.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
- His "beer and foam" analogy: a stock's price mixes fundamental operating value (beer) with speculation, hype, fear, and greed (foam) — Tesla being mostly foam in his view — and short-term trading of the foam is gambling, while long-term investing in the beer builds wealth; he also warns that advisers paid on assets under management face conflicts of interest, and that by the time you know enough to pick a good adviser you can invest on your own.[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]

# Takeaways

Collins's message, as he presents it, is that wealth is a solved problem for anyone with the discipline to want freedom more than stuff: spend less than you earn, direct the surplus into a total-market index fund inside tax-advantaged accounts, ignore the market's storms and your own urge to tinker, and let compounding run for decades — a $500 monthly investment at 8% becomes over $1 million in 35 years. The house, the Ferrari, and the trading course are, in his framing, traps that swap freedom for status. He closes with personal reflections — no regrets about paths not taken, two painful memories involving his father, and a cheerful materialist's curiosity about death — insisting that while life may have no cosmic meaning, "it's the only life you have and you might as well make the best of it."[^passive-income-expert-buying-a-house-makes-you-p-FoeQUNASmTU]
