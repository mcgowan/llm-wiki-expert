# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett interviews Paul Brunson, a professional matchmaker since 2008 who co-founded one of the largest matchmaking agencies in the United States with his wife Jill, led global research at Tinder, hosts Married at First Sight UK, and has just written the book "Keep Love", which debunks what he presents as the myths holding relationships back. Brunson argues the modern relationship landscape is polarised: citing Eli Finkel's "The All-or-Nothing Marriage", he claims roughly 20% of couples are more satisfied than at any point in history because of unprecedented tools and access to therapy, while 80% are more dissatisfied, confused and hopeless than ever — largely because we now demand that a single partner be best friend, co-CEO, co-parent and lover, roles a whole village once filled.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]

The guest's prescription is deliberately provocative: lower your expectations of your partner, involve your trusted friends and family in partner selection the way arranged-marriage cultures do, and judge relationships by satisfaction rather than longevity. He argues that attraction choices made from low self-esteem — picking partners for how they look "on your arm" — repeatedly fail, and that the best predictors of a great partner are not shared values (which change over time) but three traits: focus on their own well-being, open-mindedness, and resilience.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]

The conversation also works through a series of myths from the book — that more sex means a happier relationship, that doubts are a bad sign, that you should never go to bed angry, that total transparency is best, and that cheating must end a relationship — with Brunson repeatedly citing research from the Gottmans, Barry Schwartz, and attachment theory, and both men candidly discussing their own relationships, including Brunson's eight-year IVF journey and Bartlett's recent conflict over ignored "bids for attention."[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]

# Key points

- The guest cites cancer-caregiving research (page 48 of his book) showing a 2.9% separation rate when the man is terminally ill versus 21% when the woman is — men being roughly 600% more likely to leave a terminally ill partner — which he attributes to pre-existing low relationship satisfaction and frames as proof that love between partners is conditional, not unconditional.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- He cites James Coyne's study of 200 congenital heart disease patients: two years on, about 11% of those in high-satisfaction relationships had died versus 45% in low-satisfaction ones — more than three times the death rate — arguing relationship satisfaction dictates not just relationship survival but our own.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- Brunson argues arranged marriages often show similar or higher long-term satisfaction (the host cites a 2012 Journal of Comparative Family Studies paper) because family members vet the match objectively while the infatuated individual cannot; his matchmaking agency pioneered building client profiles by interviewing brothers, co-workers and exes rather than the client.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- Drawing on Barry Schwartz's paradox of choice, he says endless dating options make any chosen partner feel disposable — contrasting his grandmother in rural Jamaica, who had five or six options and invested deeply in one — and recommends serial daters escape their usual "market" (his "premium effect" example: sending a white north-London friend to an art exhibit at the Black Cultural Archives in Brixton).[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- On attachment: roughly 50-60% of people are secure, and anxious and avoidant types tend to pair up (anxious is comfortable pursuing, avoidant comfortable being pursued); he says an earned secure attachment is achievable, even without a therapist, and that self-awareness of your attachment style is the first step for perpetual daters who can't convert.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- His three traits of a great partner: they nurture their own well-being (referencing Carol Ryff's six dimensions of psychological well-being), they are open-minded and curious, and they are resilient — while shared values, which he traces to religious "equally yoked" teaching, are overrated because values change.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- He quotes the Gottmans' findings that "we're all compatible with each other" (a couple stranded on an island would bond), that contempt predicts divorce with over 90% accuracy, and that 69% of relationship problems are never resolved — so partners need conflict management skills, not conflict resolution, and should seek understanding rather than trying to win arguments.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- Myth-busting on sex: high satisfaction causes frequent sex, not the reverse; he distinguishes spontaneous desire (typical of men) from responsive desire (more common in women, requiring built-up "emotional currency" per Dr Karen Gurney), and notes an orgasm gap of roughly 95% for men versus about 65% for women in committed relationships, often never discussed.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- He endorses "selective disclosure" over total transparency — studies show couples who share information filtered for relevance, timing, tone and their partner's boundaries report higher satisfaction, less conflict and more autonomy — e.g., not reporting a platonic DM from an ex to a partner working through jealousy.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- Contrary to popular advice, he says you should go to bed angry: citing a study where sleep-deprived participants reported far more distress at disturbing images than those allowed eight hours' sleep, he argues sleep regulates emotion and produces better reconciliation the next morning.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- On marriage itself, he argues it should be much harder to get into (mandatory vetting and premarital counselling, not a $25 Vegas drive-through) and much easier to get out of; he predicts governments will increasingly incentivise marriage as nuclear families and birth rates decline, as seen in Singapore, China and Japan.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
- On the gender debate in the title: he acknowledges data that educated women date hypergamously and prefer taller, higher-earning men, but argues these preferences are part socialisation ("Disneyfication") handed to women, urges extending "more grace to our men" amid a male loneliness epidemic (fewer than 27% of men have a confidant), and says infidelity — including undisclosed pornography or OnlyFans use — need not end a relationship, noting Gottman-method couples reach higher satisfaction about 70% of the time after professional help.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]

# Takeaways

Brunson's closing counsel, given to his own 14-year-old son, is to surround yourself with people in healthy, satisfying relationships and absorb their habits, and his overarching message is that partner selection is "the most important decision we will make" — one that should be made from high self-esteem and well-being, with input from those who love us, and then sustained through deliberate work: emotional deposits, effective apologies (his "ARC" method — acknowledge, remorse, commitment, calibrated to the partner's threshold), normalised conversations about attraction to others, and weekly attention to the relationship itself. The host, an avowed sceptic of marriage, ends the episode re-examining his own conflict behaviour and promising to apologise to his partner that evening.[^paul-brunson-women-need-to-lower-their-standards-6ikIGGi859w]
