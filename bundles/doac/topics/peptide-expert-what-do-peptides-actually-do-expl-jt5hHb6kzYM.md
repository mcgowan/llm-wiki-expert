# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Alex Tatem, a urologist and men's health specialist, about peptides — a topic whose search volume Bartlett says has jumped 400%. Tatem explains peptides as fragments of amino-acid chains (he uses a Lego analogy) that act like precise keys for specific cellular locks, in contrast to small-molecule drugs, which he likens to hammers with wide-ranging side effects. He traces the field from insulin (the first medical peptide, isolated in 1921) through modern compounds like BPC-157 for tissue healing, GHK-Cu for skin, CJC-1295/Ipamorelin for growth-hormone release, and the GLP-1 weight-loss drugs semaglutide and tirzepatide.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]

A large part of the conversation is regulatory history as Tatem tells it: the 2013 Myriad Genetics Supreme Court ruling removed pharma's incentive to develop unpatentable natural compounds; compounding pharmacies filled the gap from 2014 until the FDA abruptly moved 19 popular peptides to its banned "category two" in 2023 — a move Tatem says came despite no adverse-event signal and which he "110%" believes reflected pharmaceutical industry pressure, since money spent on cheap compounded peptides is money not spent on patented drugs. The interview was recorded on April 15, the day the FDA announced it would consider re-legalizing seven peptides (including BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, C-Max, DSIP, and epithalon) in July.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]

Tatem is enthusiastic but repeatedly stresses trade-offs: he warns against unregulated "research use only" gray-market vials ("gas station sushi"), notes growth hormone abuse can cause insulin resistance and acromegaly, and insists peptides are tools, not shortcuts — "you still have to go to the gym." He also connects peptides to his own specialty, arguing GLP-1-driven reversal of metabolic disease could counter the documented multi-decade collapse in male sperm counts.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]

# Key points

- Tatem defines peptides as targeted amino-acid "keys" for specific cellular "locks," versus small molecules that act like hammers with broad side effects; insulin (1921) was the first medical peptide and Lupron (1985) an early urology example.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- BPC-157, a synthetic version of a gut peptide, promotes blood-vessel growth and healing — Tatem cites rat studies where surgically severed Achilles tendons healed with BPC-157, and says researchers haven't even established an LD1 (dose harming 1% of subjects) because it is so well tolerated, though human data remain limited.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- His regulatory narrative: the 2013 Myriad Genetics ruling barred patents on natural compounds, killing pharma's incentive to develop them; a 2012-13 compounding-pharmacy fungal meningitis scandal triggered FDA oversight that initially allowed peptide compounding (2014), before the FDA banned 19 popular peptides overnight in 2023 citing "insufficient data" — a move RFK Jr has characterized as illegal, per Tatem.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- Asked whether big pharma wanted peptides out of ordinary people's hands because they can't be patented, Tatem answers "110%" — though he frames pharma not as evil conspirators but as "large machines designed to prioritize profit over everything."[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- On the day of recording (April 15), the FDA announced it would consider re-legalizing seven peptides in July, including BPC-157 (healing), TB-500 (blood flow to injured tissue), KPV, MOTS-c ("exercise in a vial" — VO2 max and ATP), C-Max (cognition, studied in Russia for TBI and stroke), DSIP and epithalon (sleep/circadian regulation).[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- Since the ban, a gray market of "research use only" vendors has stepped in — Tatem compares it to Prohibition and to "gas station sushi," warning buyers can't verify contents, dosing, or endotoxin removal, and advises patients to work with doctors and legal 503A compounding pharmacies instead.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- He calls GLP-1 drugs the best current tool for insulin resistance and recounts a patient whose sperm count rose tenfold into the normal range after losing 100 lb on tirzepatide plus diet and exercise; Tatem himself takes a low dose of tirzepatide, the only peptide he currently uses because it's the only one legally available to him.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- Compounded tirzepatide (with niacinamide) allows flexible microdosing that can reduce side effects, but Tatem says Lilly and Novo Nordisk lobbying has driven an FDA crackdown on compounders — he notes FDA commissioner Marty Makary has tweeted more about compounded GLP-1s than about diabetes or heart disease.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
- Trade-offs he flags: growth hormone (somatotropin) excess can cause insulin resistance, acromegaly's irreversible facial-bone changes, numb hands from joint effusions, and could theoretically worsen an existing cancer; melanotan II tans skin (and produces dramatic erections) but carries melanoma concerns; rapid GLP-1 weight loss cannibalizes muscle, which upcoming myostatin-inhibitor antibodies (bimagrumab, roledumab, trevogrumab) aim to prevent.[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]

# Takeaways

Tatem's overall case is that peptides are a legitimate, versatile class of medicine — "the question isn't what can peptides do, it's what can't they do" — that was caught between patent law, FDA caution, and pharmaceutical lobbying, and is now returning to legality amid enormous public demand. His consistent caveats are that gray-market products are unsafe, that every compound carries trade-offs, and that no peptide replaces training, diet, and a collaborative relationship with a doctor: patients should start from their problem, not from the drug. He frames the stakes as societal, arguing that metabolic disease underlies most chronic illness — from heart disease to infertility to erectile dysfunction — and that these tools should be available to everyone, "not just the billionaires."[^peptide-expert-what-do-peptides-actually-do-expl-jt5hHb6kzYM]
