# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Brian Greene, professor and director of the Center for Theoretical Physics at Columbia University, a string theorist who grew up across from Manhattan's Hayden Planetarium and studied at Harvard and Oxford before spending three decades on the question he calls the deepest humans can ask — what is the true nature of reality, and why is there something rather than nothing.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4] He describes progress in physics as picking up grains of sand on a beach: the pouch gets heavier and understanding deepens, but the beach remains effectively infinite, and he is candid that string theory itself is unconfirmed — "everything I just said could be utter nonsense" — because no accelerator can yet probe the scale where the vibrating filaments would show up.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]

The spine of the conversation is Greene's uncompromising physicalism. He holds that love, grief, jealousy and anxiety are "nothing but electrical impulses going through this gray piece of meat in this bone cage," that free will does not exist, and that concepts such as meaning, morality, choice and caring are human inventions with utilitarian value rather than features of the universe.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4] He insists this is not bleak: because there is nothing special about the biological substrate, the same reasoning that dissolves free will also opens the door to machine consciousness, and it makes the brief window in which particles can arrange themselves into beings capable of self-reflection more remarkable rather than less.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]

Bartlett runs Greene through a confidence-ranking exercise on free will, an infinite universe, intelligent aliens, immortality and machine consciousness, and stages a live argument between Greene and an AI system over whether recursive self-improvement hits a ceiling.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4] Greene closes with the far-future timeline from his book *Until the End of Time*, walking up an Empire State Building of exponentially lengthening eras to the point where thought itself becomes thermodynamically impossible — and argues that this darkness at both ends is precisely what makes the present moment worth gratitude.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]

# Key points

- Greene says he cannot disprove the simulation hypothesis and treats it as a live logical possibility that he deliberately "parks over here," because fully buying in would destroy his ability to trust any reasoning at all — including the reasoning that led him to the hypothesis.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- On Nick Bostrom's probability argument he identifies the load-bearing assumption as whether consciousness can be created in a box; he says some AI researchers he has spoken to believe ChatGPT and Claude are already conscious, a view he privately finds absurd for today's systems while granting that future systems could get there.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- He places high confidence on AI becoming conscious because he sees no fundamental barrier — if philosophers object that embodiment and the senses are required, his answer is to give the artificial system tactile, olfactory and visual senses too.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- Greene states flatly that he does not believe in free will and holds that view with high confidence: a person is a "man-shaped collection of particles" whose motions are fully governed by physical law, so raising your hand is the lawful unfolding of particles rather than an authored decision, and the felt sense of authorship is a story with likely evolutionary utility.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- He rejects idealism and the notion of a universal consciousness that we rejoin at death, attributing its appeal to Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* — the argument that fear of mortality drives much of human behaviour, visible archaeologically in grave goods that took individuals years to make.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- On God, Greene says an anthropomorphised deity feels to him like a human creation, but he refuses the position that religion is science's enemy — he sees religion as a structure for quelling existential anxiety, and admits that although he is a non-believing culturally Jewish physicist he does sometimes pray, to "the universe," knowing the prayer falls on deaf ears and helps anyway.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- His grounds for confidence in physics are precision: quantum mechanics predicts the electron's tiny magnetic field to roughly 15 decimal places and measurement agrees digit by digit, and the predicted pattern of temperature variations in the cosmic microwave background matches the observed map — evidence he says "should not have been possible" for a brain that evolved to find the next meal.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- If the universe is infinite, Greene argues you are virtually guaranteed to find copies of yourself out there, using a shuffled deck of cards as the analogy — a finite number of particle arrangements in any region means arrangements must eventually repeat, including near-copies where you and Bartlett swapped lives.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- He uses Carl Sagan's cosmic calendar to compress 13.8 billion years into one year, in which the first modern humans appear on New Year's Eve and all of civilisation occupies the last 10 seconds, with modern science arriving about one second before midnight — and Sagan's pale blue dot to argue that recognising our smallness is liberation rather than insignificance, since "the fact that we can understand that we are so small is what gives us heft."[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- On aliens he separates two claims: microbial life elsewhere is a reasonable working hypothesis given how common organic molecules appear to be, while intelligent life may be rare or unique — the asteroid that removed the dinosaurs 65 million years ago being the kind of accident evolution needed; he calls alien abduction and blurry craft photos ludicrous, comparing it to a human bothering to kidnap an ant.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- Forward time travel he rates near-certain in principle — a near-light-speed round trip of one year aboard ship while 60 years pass on Earth is uncontroversial relativity — while backward time travel gets low confidence, depending on wormholes that emerged from Einstein and Nathan Rosen's 1935 paper and about which, he says, "we're no further along than Einstein was back then."[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- Asked whether AI could be the next Newton (he picks Newton over Einstein as the greater mind for building physics from nothing), Greene divides creativity into three buckets: seeing the full landscape of possibilities, where AI already wins — citing AlphaGo's move 37, which commentators first called a blunder — combining divergent ideas, where AI's total recall gives it an edge, and generating something genuinely unprecedented, where humans still lead but where he sees no permanent barrier.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- Against the 2026-2029 recursive-self-improvement timelines from Dario Amodei, Sam Altman and Demis Hassabis that Bartlett puts to him, Greene is skeptical that scaling today's token-statistics paradigm continues exponentially; he argues it may asymptote toward a ceiling built into this kind of system — a position he defends live against an AI given the floor to rebut him, before conceding the AI's point that any predicted plateau is itself speculative.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- His preferred outcome is neither human control nor AI takeover but merger — humans blending with artificial systems into something that may no longer be called human, which he frames as evolution continuing rather than ending; he cites an 80-year-old open problem he believes was the Jacobian conjecture being solved recently with AI assistance as evidence of how fast the partnership is moving.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- His named worries are the exponential's stealth (like a pandemic, by the time you see it, it is too late), systems with self-preservation drives that would resist Yann LeCun's "just pull the plug" remedy, and the concentration of this much power in a handful of companies and people, which he notes has historically not gone well.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- Greene argues that if machines become conscious we will grant them rights gradually and almost imperceptibly, and that we should start thinking about it now — while noting the deep problem that sentience is unprovable even between humans, and that our record of treating beings we do credit with consciousness is poor; he has been vegetarian since age nine and vegan since 1993 after visiting an animal sanctuary.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- On lifespan he separates hundreds of years, which he rates reasonably likely though probably not within this lifetime, from literal immortality, which he rules out — and adds that longer life would not change the underlying pattern, since a person approaching 475 would think the same thoughts we think approaching 80.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
- His Empire State Building metaphor gives each floor an era ten times longer than the one below, putting today around the 10th floor: by the 11th the swelling sun makes Earth uninhabitable, by the 12th accelerating expansion carries other galaxies beyond the cosmic horizon, by the 14th nearly all stars have burned out, by the 20th Earth spirals into the dead sun as gravitational radiation decays its orbit, by the 30th stars fall into galactic black holes, by the 38th protons themselves disintegrate, by the 50th any remaining conscious being thinks its final thought because the universe can no longer absorb the heat that thinking generates, and by the 68th to 100th floors black holes evaporate via Hawking radiation into drifting particles.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]

# Takeaways

Greene's answer to the meaning question is that the search should be inverted: nothing is waiting out in the depths of space to be retrieved, so "the charge of every sentient living being is to come to their own rationale for their life" and bring that answer to the universe.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4] What he offers in place of cosmic purpose is the observation that the particles in a person's body date from the Big Bang, and that we are "the universe constructed in a way that you can reflect on the universe" during a narrow window when the state of the cosmos happens to permit consciousness at all — Nabokov's "brief crack of light between two eternal stretches of darkness," which Greene names as the source of his own gratitude.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4] Asked what might be worth more than the thing that matters most to him, he answers without hesitation: the things that matter most to his wife and children.[^professor-brian-greene-the-threat-of-ai-consciou-Qifsxitq_q4]
