# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Anne Applebaum, the Pulitzer Prize-winning historian of the Soviet Union and author of Twilight of Democracy, who argues that in the modern world democracies rarely end with tanks in the street — they mostly end when a legitimately elected leader begins to take apart the neutral institutions (independent courts, electoral commissions, media, and a meritocratic civil service) that make free elections possible.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA] She traces the playbook to Viktor Orban's Hungary and contends that the second Trump administration is following it: she notes that democracy indexes no longer classify the United States as a liberal democracy, and points to Trump's net worth reportedly rising from $2.3 billion to $6.5 billion in two years in office as evidence of an unprecedented, kleptocratic merger of presidential power and family business.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]

The core of the conversation is Applebaum's five tactics by which elected autocrats dismantle democracies — high-level corruption, manipulation of elections, politicization of the civil service, control of the information space, and control of coercive power — each of which she illustrates with contemporary American examples, from gerrymandering and restrictive voter-ID proposals to pressure on the Fed and media ownership, to ICE operating as a masked, unaccountable paramilitary force.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA] She also situates the moment globally: the post-1945 order is breaking down, autocracies like Russia and China are waging a war of ideas against liberal-democratic language, and America's allies — shaken especially by Trump's threats against Greenland and Denmark — are now hedging with new trade, security, and even nuclear arrangements that exclude the US.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]

Against Bartlett's interest in cyclical theories of imperial decline, Applebaum insists she does not believe in historical inevitability: complacency after the Cold War helped democracies slip, and whether the United States survives as a democracy depends on choices citizens make now — voting, civic participation, and defending institutions and truth-seeking journalism.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]

# Key points

- The guest argues modern democracies mostly end when legitimately elected leaders dismantle the system from within — the pioneer being Viktor Orban, who used a two-thirds parliamentary majority to repeatedly rewrite Hungary's constitution for electoral advantage.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- She notes that on current democracy maps the United States is no longer counted as a liberal democracy but as a less-free "electoral democracy," and warns the US could become a de facto one-party state — a condition with American precedent in the pre-civil-rights South.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- On corruption: Trump's net worth reportedly grew from $2.3 billion to $6.5 billion in two years in office; she cites the Trump family's Saudi property deals and the Saudi government's $2 billion investment in Jared Kushner's fund — Kushner now negotiating in the Middle East with his own business partners — as conflicts of interest without precedent in American history.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- She describes the Hungarian method of state capture over businesses — broken windows, harassed children, tax inspections until the owner sells — and agrees with the host's comparison to the administration publicly threatening Anthropic after it refused certain government conditions for its AI.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- She explains tech CEOs' sycophancy toward Trump as pay-to-play, calling it shortsighted — "I'm sick of these oligarchs, I want different oligarchs" is what happened in Russia — and argues firms and law firms that refused to settle or capitulate have thrived.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Tactic two, elections: gerrymandering, fears of ICE deployment at polling places, and voter-ID proposals requiring passports or birth certificates — which the host notes would disqualify roughly 24% of voters aged 18-29, 11% of citizens of color, and affect 69 million married women whose birth certificates don't match their legal names — built on an evidence-free narrative of illegal-immigrant voting.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Tactic three, personnel: replacing expert, independent civil servants with loyalists — she cites the takeover of the Department of Justice to prosecute the president's enemies and pressure on Fed chairman Jerome Powell via a manufactured investigation.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Tactic four, information: modern censorship works through media ownership rather than crossed-out sentences — as in Orban's Hungary and Erdogan's Turkey — and she sees Trump attempting the same via pressure on TikTok, CBS, and CNN acquisitions, plus unprecedented interference such as trying to dictate courses and hiring at Harvard.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Tactic five, coercive power: she describes ICE as a masked, militarized national police force accountable only to the president, and says the administration's reaction to two US citizens killed during Minnesota protests — instant impunity rather than investigation — marks it as a paramilitary above the law.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Globally, she argues autocrats fear the language of liberal democracy most — Putin's greatest fear being a 2014 Ukraine-style street revolution — which is why Russia invaded Ukraine and why autocracies run disinformation campaigns selling stability, hierarchy, and traditional values inside Western democracies.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- She reports allies worldwide hedging against an unreliable America — an EU-India trade agreement, Canada-EU security ties, Franco-Polish-German nuclear-umbrella talks — and identifies the Greenland episode, in which Denmark actually planned for a US invasion, as Europe's breaking point.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
- Presented with Sir John Glubb's 250-year empire life-cycle theory (which would place the US, founded 1776, in its "age of decadence" in 2026), she rejects historical inevitability as dangerous: believing decline is inevitable — or that victory is — takes away the will to act, and countries like Poland show trajectories can change.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]

# Takeaways

Applebaum's closing counsel is that democracy's survival is a matter of choices, not cycles: citizens should vote in every election including local ones, resist the nihilism ("they're all the same") that autocrats deliberately cultivate, and watch how leaders talk about the press, judges, and civil service — a real democrat respects the institutions that guarantee the next fair election.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA] She urges people to imagine concretely what regime change would feel like — waking up in a society where free speech is presumed bad and advancement requires a cousin in the ruling party — because understanding what would be lost is the first defense against losing it, and she stresses that truth-seeking journalism must survive for any meaningful national conversation to exist.[^pulitzer-historian-the-next-stage-is-democracy-c-kwEtOyaFhCA]
