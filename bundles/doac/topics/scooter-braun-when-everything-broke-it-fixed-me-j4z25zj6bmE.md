# Overview

In this Diary Of A CEO conversation, Steven Bartlett interviews Scooter Braun — the manager behind Justin Bieber, Ariana Grande, and many other artists, and the entrepreneur who sold his company for a reported $1.1 billion. Braun's central confession is that "Scooter" was a character: born Scott, first-generation American son of a Hungarian refugee father and grandson of Holocaust survivors, he says he created the Scooter persona as an adult because he didn't believe Scott could achieve big things, and it took intense therapy at 40 for him to "fall in love with his name again" and recognize that his drive came from shame about his privilege and a deep fear of not being enough.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]

The episode traces his rise — selling fake IDs and promoting parties at Emory, dropping out after a dean's "dream buster" speech, becoming Jermaine Dupri's youngest VP at So So Def at 20, founding SB Projects at 24, and discovering a 13-year-old Justin Bieber on YouTube and tracking him down within 24 hours by calling Canadian school boards. He revisits the Taylor Swift/Big Machine catalog controversy, which he frames as a painful but transformative "gift": the moment he learned that the praise he'd received wasn't deserved and neither was the hate, because neither camp actually knew him.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]

The emotional core is his account of October 2020: with his marriage collapsing while the world thought he was "crushing it," he experienced a 20-minute suicidal thought, then chose to attend the Hoffman Process during the busiest work week of his year. He argues the subsequent losses — divorce, retiring from management after 23 years, public vilification — broke the mask and fixed the man, and he closes by turning the interviewer's chair around, pressing Bartlett on his own workaholism and relationship, and admitting that fear of failure has kept him from writing a book.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]

# Key points

- Braun says he created "Scooter" as a mask because he didn't think "Scott" could achieve his ambitions, and only understood this at 40 through intense therapy; his father told him and his brother every night, "You're different, you're special, I hold you to a higher standard."[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He revises his famous baseball analogy about success (staying at the plate and swinging while the crowd boos): he now argues the booing crowd was never the outside naysayers — it is the voice in your own head telling you you're not enough, a "deep, deep lie" even the most confident people carry.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- At 14 his father caught him in a lie and, instead of punishing him, told him he was good enough at lying to be successful — "but you'll be a liar, and I'll know, and you'll know" — a lesson Braun credits with his commitment to integrity.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He attributes his early breaks (Jermaine Dupri, Ludacris, Usher) to respect for elders, letting his work attract attention rather than asking for opportunities, and — repeatedly, in his own word — "ignorance": no one told him he shouldn't be there, and a lack of limiting beliefs functioned like belief.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He found Justin Bieber at 25 by watching church videos posted by Bieber's mother, tracked the family down within 24 hours via the church's background and Canadian school boards, and convinced them to take the first flight of their lives to Atlanta; he grew Bieber from roughly 60,000 to 60 million YouTube views when "YouTubers don't turn into musicians" was the consensus.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- Asked what his artists have in common, he answers "pain," and expresses guilt about managing young stars before he'd done his own inner work; he says he would have put a therapist on the road and slowed everyone down, and argues "human beings are not made to be worshipped, we're made to serve."[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He tells the story of Spencer Lee, whom he calls maybe the most talented artist he ever signed, who wrote the addiction song "River Water," relapsed, and died of an overdose — and shares the Al-Anon lesson he learned: "be a rock," a constant home an addict can return to, while credit for beating addiction belongs to the addict alone.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- On the Taylor Swift/Big Machine episode, he says they had met only three or four times, he expected to work together after the acquisition, was shocked by her Tumblr post, offered to sell her the catalog multiple times (which he says is now documented), and ultimately sold it to someone else; he frames the global hate as the gift of learning that neither the prior praise nor the subsequent hate was deserved.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He recounts a 20-minute suicidal thought in October 2020 when his marriage was failing — "if I can't be this perfect image, I don't want to be here" — and credits the Hoffman Process (a week of no-phone early-childhood work) with the result that he was never depressed again, even as divorce followed; six years on, divorced and out of management, he says he "couldn't be happier."[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- He shares Berry Gordy's Motown 25 story and its lesson — "it will never end the way you want it to, but it doesn't mean it didn't happen" — as the frame for retiring from management after 23 years, and cites the Cavafy poem "Ithaca" (the namesake of his holding company, given to him by David Geffen) as the journey-over-destination philosophy he lives by.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- At 27 he cold-called everyone on the Billboard 30 under 30 list to build peer community, which led to meeting Daniel Ek and becoming an early Spotify investor who has never sold a share; he argues Ek "saved the music industry" by restoring value to catalogs and masters through streaming.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
- Other frameworks he offers: amor fati (his tattoo — love your fate, including the pain), "surrender and participate" rather than control, the shift from a competitive mind (finite) to a curious and creative mind (infinite), Jeff Bezos's advice to "harmonize" rather than balance, and the Kabbalah idea of being a custodian of wealth rather than an owner.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]

# Takeaways

Braun's message is that rock bottom was the correction his success never provided: the public backlash, the divorce, and losing half his time with his kids forced him to drop the mask, reconnect with childhood friends who "knew Scott, not Scooter," and stop spending energy impressing people who didn't love him instead of the many who already did. He turns the lesson directly on the host — telling Bartlett to "turn the cameras off and go do some self-work" because there is no perfect future time — and argues the things people actually want at 90 (partner, children, friends) are usually already in hand, while the building should return to being what it was in childhood: joy, not proof of worth.[^scooter-braun-when-everything-broke-it-fixed-me-j4z25zj6bmE]
