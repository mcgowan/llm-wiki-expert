# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Esther Perel, the couples therapist and author (Mating in Captivity, The State of Affairs) with 40 years of practice, about what most concerns her in modern relationships. Her answer is "social atrophy": like unused muscles, social skills waste away when people pursue connection through screens instead of humans, and because we are wired for connection — she argues we live longer because of relationships, not biohacking — the cost is existential. Dating apps, she says, began as a beautiful way to broaden one's circle but became "emotional capitalism" and commodification, where semi-anonymity licenses ghosting and rudeness; the real misery is not failing to meet someone but the treatment people endure, and the answer is to treat the app as a tool while creating real-world situations to meet people and learning to tolerate rejection, which she calls a major feature of relationships.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]

Perel connects this atrophy directly to the "sexual recession": statistics Bartlett cites — one in three US men under 30 reporting no sex in the past year (triple the 2008 rate), over 40% of young Japanese adults virgins, declining marital sex since the early 2000s — reflect, in her reading, people who have sex on porn rather than partnered sex, and young men in their 20s now forming the majority of erectile-dysfunction cases in sex therapy because solo porn use never trains the attunement partnered sex requires. For couples, she introduces "ambiguous loss" (Pauline Boss's term for a person physically present but psychologically absent, as with dementia): the partner scrolling beside you on the sofa produces the same not-here-not-gone confusion, and dead bedrooms are downstream of lives that have stopped being interesting to each other.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]

The episode also features live "interventions" on anonymous questions from Bartlett's friends — on confessing infidelity, lost attraction, and monogamous boredom — plus Perel's critiques of self-care culture and the gender wars, her account of trauma as the absence of an empathic witness, and her four relational pillars for the workplace developed with Culture Amp.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]

# Key points

- Perel's core diagnosis is "social atrophy": social skills decay without practice, we have ever fewer chances to practice because we pursue connection beyond the human world, and since connection is literally vital — social creatures live longer because of it — the loss compounds into loneliness, sexlessness, and self-doubt.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- Discussing the man who swiped on Tinder over 2 million times across nearly six years for 2,000 matches, 1,200 chats, and one date, she says she would not discuss what he does on the app but off it — "more of it isn't going to give you more of it" — and urges creating real situations: walk the dog, join a bike group, offer the person next to you a coffee.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- She argues dating apps were originally designed to spare users rejection, but rejection is a "major feature" of relationships that must be practiced; the deeper harm of app culture is commodification — ghosting and the loss of minimum decency is what makes people bitter, not merely failing to match.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- She warns that algorithmic perfection and sycophantic AI responses create warped expectations: the more we interact with frictionless, on-demand technologies, the harder real people — who refuse, disagree, and disappoint — become to tolerate.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- On the sexual recession, she notes the majority of men coming to sex therapy for erectile dysfunction today are in their 20s, not post-prostate older men, because porn-plus-masturbation trains self-attunement rather than the resonance ("grooving together") partnered sex requires.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- She applies Pauline Boss's concept of "ambiguous loss" to phones: a partner physically present but psychologically elsewhere produces the same confusion as dementia or a deployed loved one — "are you here or are you not?" — and she prescribes carving out clean, demarcated, phone-free time, which benefits the distracted partner's own wellbeing as much as the relationship.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- On a friend's confession question, she argues honesty can be cruel as well as caring: confessing an old infidelity to cleanse your own guilt can rob the partner of their narrative; sometimes the responsible path is to carry the guilt, face the consequences privately, and invest in making the relationship the best it can be.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- She claims women get bored with monogamy sooner than men — the opposite of the popular assumption — because for women to want sex, it must be sex worth wanting; what reads as "she's not interested in sex" is usually "she's not interested in the sex on offer."[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- Her recurring formula: attraction and desire are consequences of the relationship's aliveness — "the sex is the consequence of seven other things before" — so couples who don't talk, laugh, kiss, or look at each other cannot expect passion on demand; the question is what you do to bring playfulness, curiosity, and eroticism (as life force) rather than complaining.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- She critiques self-care culture as having "gone overboard" into consumerism and navel-gazing: relating to yourself is a step, not a goal; wellbeing, happiness, and longevity are rooted in relationships and in giving to others, and self-awareness itself develops through connection — "it is in the presence of another that we discover ourselves."[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- On men and masculinity: she argues male loneliness is cultural, not biological (19th-century men hunted, hiked, and talked together; boys are highly emotional until social conditioning), notes "emasculation" and "loser" have no feminine equivalents because masculinity is an identity that constantly must prove itself, and declines to feed the gender wars, describing retreat "fishbowls" where men speak and women listen without judgment — "humanity is bigger than gender."[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
- Drawing on Howard Markman's work, she says couples' fights are really about three things — power and control, trust and closeness, and respect and recognition — and her Culture Amp collaboration (drawing on some 1.5 billion survey data points) found four relational pillars at work: trust, belonging, recognition, and collective resilience, which she calls "the new bottom line."[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]

# Takeaways

Perel's prescription is deliberately small-scale: talk to strangers, say hello, sing with people ("anybody who can make people laugh or sing together is doing holy work"), send sweet nothings during the day, and start meetings or evenings with real questions — because "life is lived in the details" and, in the line she carries everywhere, "the quality of your relationships will determine the quality of your lives." She defines confidence (via Terry Real) as seeing yourself as flawed and still holding yourself in high regard, argues that what most often turns adversity into resilience rather than collapse is one person who believed in you, and reframes trauma as an event suffered without an empathic witness — closing with hope that AI remains a tool that people shape rather than a force that reshapes our imperfect, fascinating species.[^sex-expert-esther-perel-the-relationship-crisis-QCaFWrT0j-g]
