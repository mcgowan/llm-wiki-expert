# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett talks with Dr. David Eagleman, the Stanford neuroscientist and author of Livewired, about brain plasticity — the brain's lifelong ability to rewire itself — and what it means for self-change, aging, dreams, and AI. Eagleman describes the brain as a "team of rivals": competing neural networks with different drives whose "neural parliament" votes determine behavior, which is why he recommends Ulysses contracts — pre-commitments that constrain your future self, like an alcoholic clearing the house of alcohol — as a practical tool for aligning actions with intentions.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]

Eagleman argues the brain peaks in connectivity around age two and then prunes; adults trade fluid intelligence for crystallized intelligence, and plasticity declines mainly because a well-fitted internal model no longer needs to change. The remedy is deliberately seeking challenge in the "frustrating but achievable" zone, dropping skills once mastered, and staying socially engaged — he cites the religious orders study of Catholic nuns whose brains showed physical Alzheimer's degeneration without cognitive symptoms because constant social and mental challenge had built "cognitive reserve."[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]

The conversation's headline claim is Eagleman's theory of why we dream: because the planet rotates into darkness, the visual cortex risks being colonized by the other senses (a Harvard blindfolding experiment showed takeover beginning within 60 minutes), so every ~90 minutes the midbrain blasts random activity into the visual system — REM sleep — purely to defend that territory. The episode closes on AI: Eagleman is a self-described "cyber optimist" who sees AI as a "motorcycle for the mind," distinguishes vicious from virtuous friction, and predicts a renaissance of in-person human experiences.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]

# Key points

- Eagleman describes the brain's 86 billion neurons as organized into competing networks — a "team of rivals" — so the self is better understood as "know thyselves"; Ulysses contracts (pre-commitments made now to block bad future behavior) are his key tool for self-control.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- He says the brain peaks in neural connections at age two, then prunes toward crystallized intelligence; plasticity declines not because it must but because a good internal model needs less updating — so adults can still change by seeking novelty and challenge at the edge of frustration.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- Critical windows matter: children who never learn language early (he cites Romanian orphans raised without being held or spoken to after Ceausescu's fall) never acquire the concept of language and suffer lasting cognitive deficits.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- The religious orders study found nuns whose autopsied brains showed Alzheimer's pathology but who had shown no cognitive symptoms in life — Eagleman attributes this "cognitive reserve" to lifelong social challenge, chores, games, and disputes in the convent, and warns that passive retirement and shrinking social circles accelerate decline, since "nothing is as hard for the brain as other people."[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- Experience visibly reshapes cortex: pianists develop enlarged motor-cortex regions for both hands while violinists show it in one hemisphere, and the cortex is a "one-trick pony" whose territory is reallocated — visual cortex is taken over in the born-blind, auditory cortex in the born-deaf.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- Novices show widespread brain activity while experts show little for the same task, because the brain's goal is to burn practiced skills into efficient circuitry; his dementia-prevention advice is to drop skills once mastered and take on things you are bad at, continually building new neural "roadways" so degeneration leaves alternate routes intact.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- His dream theory: because Earth rotates into darkness, dreaming exists to defend the visual cortex from sensory takeover — Harvard colleagues showed the takeover starting after just 60 minutes of blindfolding sighted people — so the midbrain fires random activity into the visual system every ~90 minutes during REM, which the storytelling brain weaves into narratives that are mostly meaningless.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- Supporting evidence he cites: across 25 primate species, brain plasticity correlates "perfectly" with amount of REM sleep; human infants spend about 50% of sleep in REM and it declines with age; all animals dream, including blind mole rats whose dream circuitry outlived their vision.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- On AI and learning, he distinguishes "vicious friction" (busywork worth outsourcing) from "virtuous friction" (hard thinking that builds the brain), warns that copy-pasting AI answers yields no benefit, and recommends using AI as an always-available tutor — "Aristotle in your pocket" — including asking it to be brutally honest about your blind spots.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- He is a "cyber optimist" about children and the internet: curiosity-driven, on-demand answers stick neurologically better than "just in case" schooling, though he cautions no proper control group exists for studying social media's effects on young brains.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- On AI's limits, he argues AI is massively creative at remixing but poor at selection (predicting what humans will love), exhibits "jagged intelligence," lacks one-trial learning and inside knowledge of what being human is like — though Bartlett counters with examples of Gemini perfectly predicting YouTube retention drop-offs and thumbnail A/B winners.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
- On individual differences, he discusses the aphantasia-hyperphantasia spectrum (he has aphantasia, as does Pixar founder Ed Catmull and many top Pixar animators) and synesthesia (at least 3% of people), noting these differences translate to essentially no difference in real-world capability; he closes urging "complexification" of in-group/out-group relationships to keep the brain's person-perceiving social circuitry engaged and reduce polarization.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]

# Takeaways

Eagleman's central message is that we can be "the sculptors of our own brains": because plasticity persists for life, the person you are is not the person you must remain — but change requires engineered commitment devices, genuine effort, and a steady diet of novel, difficult, and social challenges rather than willpower alone. His dream theory reframes REM sleep as nightly territorial defense of the visual cortex rather than hidden messages, and his AI stance is that those who use AI for virtuous friction — curiosity, counterargument, and learning — will pull ahead, while human connection, live experience, and seeing out-groups as people become more valuable, not less, in an AI-saturated world.[^stanford-neuroscientist-cant-remember-your-dream-m-nnyNZ0TQ0]
