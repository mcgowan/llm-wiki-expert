# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, Steven Bartlett interviews Ben Felix, the engineer-turned-portfolio-manager whose firm PWL Capital manages money for more than 3,000 people and whose approach is to translate academic finance research into practical advice. Felix's core thesis is that "investing has been solved" — use low-cost index funds and capture market returns — and that the hard part is psychological: human brains built for survival struggle with long-term abstractions, and he cites research showing that the more often people check their portfolios, the less risk they take and the lower the returns they earn.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]

A major segment tackles renting versus owning a home, which Felix calls the biggest financial decision most households make. He itemizes the "unrecoverable costs" of ownership — mortgage interest, opportunity cost of equity, property taxes, maintenance (which he says people underestimate most), emergency and renovation spending — and presents his "5% rule": divide a home's price by 5% and by 12 to find the monthly rent at which renting and owning are financially equivalent. He argues homeownership mainly suits the very risk-averse, people certain to stay put, and highly taxed investors, while renting preserves mobility and frees capital for the stock market.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]

The conversation also runs through Felix's top-ten list of financial mistakes, the PERMA model from positive psychology for setting meaningful financial goals, a controversial life-cycle asset allocation paper favoring 100% equities, products to avoid (covered-call ETFs, thematic ETFs, crypto), and why efficient markets mean stock-picking and market-timing are losing games for almost everyone — including professionals.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]

# Key points

- Felix says investing is solved — low-cost index funds — and psychology is the bottleneck: an academic paper shows the more people look at their investments, the less risk they take and the lower their returns, so his practical advice is to buy, hold, and stop checking the app.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- He argues people who know "just a little bit" — enough to trust index funds — outperform those who know "enough to hurt themselves" by researching sectors and picking stocks.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- Counterintuitively, he cites research suggesting young people may not need to save as much as social pressure implies — the principle is to save more when income is high and less when it is low — though he warns this can breed bad habits if the saving shift never comes.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- His top financial mistakes include not earning enough (fixable by investing in human capital and rare, complementary skill stacks sold in the right market), not saving enough once income rises, overspending on things that don't serve your life goals, not taking equity risk, and taking the wrong risks (stock-picking, options, crypto trading).[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- The opportunity-cost math the pair run: $10,000 invested at a 7% expected stock return becomes about $150,000 over 40 years — so a $10 daily-style coffee habit or a $10,000 purchase carries a large hidden long-term cost, tempered by the PERMA framework so present enjoyment still counts.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- He uses the PERMA model (positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, accomplishment) from positive psychology as a categorical prompt to elicit higher-quality financial goals, arguing few people ever step back to define what a good life looks like for them.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- On housing, his 5% rule (roughly 1% property taxes + 1% maintenance + 3% cost of capital) gives the break-even rent: a $300,000 house is equivalent to about $1,250/month rent — and he now thinks maintenance costs of 2%+ make even that estimate low; controlled studies (including one by Statistics Canada) find homeowners are not happier than renters once property type and neighborhood are accounted for.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- He warns homeownership limits mobility — citing Toronto's condo price collapse and Canada's biggest inflation-adjusted real-estate drawdown since 1975 — and says comparing a mortgage payment to rent is the biggest rent-vs-own mistake because it ignores unrecoverable costs.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- The "most controversial paper in finance" he discusses simulated a million lifetimes from data across 39 countries back to 1890 and found the optimal lifetime portfolio was 100% equities — roughly one-third domestic, two-thirds international — contradicting conventional glide-path advice, because bonds get "decimated" in high-inflation periods; his own money is in globally diversified stocks, and he holds no crypto beyond $1,000 each of Bitcoin and Ethereum bought for research.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- Products he tells people to avoid: covered-call ETFs (income-bias marketing that hides enormous forgone upside), thematic ETFs (launched after asset prices in a hot theme have already run up, with typically poor subsequent returns), and hard-to-access private-company products with high fees; he also notes cash loses roughly half its purchasing power over 20 years at 3% inflation.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- On relationships and money, he cites research that "tightwads" and "spendthrifts" are more likely to marry each other than their own type yet report lower marital satisfaction and more money conflict; he endorses wills for anyone with dependents ("everybody has a will — the government's default"), considered prenups, term life and disability insurance against catastrophic risk, and basic use of tax-advantaged accounts (401k/IRA, ISA, RRSP/TFSA).[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
- On markets and AI: he explains efficient-market logic (today's price already reflects what everyone knows, so "you better know something no one else knows"), notes professional managers who beat the market rarely repeat it — recalling Buffett's winning 10-year index-fund bet against hedge funds — points to data that women outperform men largely by trading less, and, citing the ATM/bank-teller story, the Jevons paradox, and Carlota Perez's work on technological revolutions, argues AI bubbles may deflate but diversified investors shouldn't try to time it since stock returns have stayed positive through history's turmoil.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]

# Takeaways

Felix's message is that building wealth is procedurally simple but behaviorally hard: define your goals with something like the PERMA model, invest early and consistently in globally diversified low-cost index funds, avoid products engineered to exploit your biases, insure catastrophic risks, use tax shelters, and then leave the portfolio alone — even when headlines scream crisis, as they have since at least 1847. He contends the rent-versus-buy decision should be made on total unrecoverable costs and life circumstances rather than the mortgage-versus-rent comparison, and that focusing on what you can control — plan, asset allocation, savings, taxes, behavior — beats every attempt to outsmart an efficient market.[^stock-expert-becoming-rich-is-simple-but-you-won-jLFG_FZKbks]
