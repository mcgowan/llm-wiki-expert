# Overview

David Raichlen is a professor of human evolutionary biology, exercise physiology and
neuroscience at the University of Southern California whose research asks how physical
activity and lifestyle behaviour shape brain health. He frames the stakes demographically:
roughly 6 million Americans currently live with Alzheimer's or a related dementia, a
figure he says is projected to reach about 13 million within 25 years and 150 million
worldwide within 30 years — and around 40% of dementia risk, he argues, is preventable
and attributable to lifestyle.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]

His central explanatory move is evolutionary. Raichlen argues that around two million
years ago humans shifted from an ape-like lifestyle to hunting and gathering, pushing
daily travel distances to 20km or more, and that human physiology was tuned over that
span to high, consistent activity — meaning inactivity is the abnormal condition, and one
we have had only a few hundred years since the Industrial Revolution to adapt to, which
he says is nowhere near enough time.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
The body, in his framing, is an energy minimiser: unused muscle, cardiovascular tissue
and apparently brain tissue all get downgraded because maintaining them costs energy the
body has been signalled it does not need.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]

Much of the episode is spent on what Raichlen calls the most dangerous misunderstanding
in his field — how much exercise people believe is required. He argues the largest public
health returns come at the very bottom of the dose curve, from people going from almost
nothing to a few thousand steps a day, and that framing activity as a requirement rather
than something that improves the day is itself a barrier.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
Alongside that he makes the case that sitting, rather than exercise, may be the more
tractable lever, since messaging on exercise has failed to move US participation for two
decades.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]

# Key points

- Raichlen reports that his group's large study found sitting 10 hours a day rather than
  9 carries roughly a 10% higher dementia risk, while 12 hours a day carries about a 60%
  higher risk than nine and a half — a non-linear, exponential relationship that kicks in
  past about nine to nine and a half hours.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- On mechanism, he says exercise raises cerebral blood flow and that working muscles
  release proteins called myokines which travel to the brain and upregulate brain-derived
  neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which acts, in his phrase, like fertiliser for new neurons —
  helping them be born, survive and integrate.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- The clearest neurogenesis evidence comes from rodents given running wheels or
  treadmills, where new hippocampal neurons appear and survive; in humans he says the
  effects are broader but measured volumetrically, showing preserved volume in the
  hippocampus and in frontal lobes tied to planning and decision-making.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- He cites Gerd Kempermann's mouse work in which combining a running wheel with an
  enriched cage doubled the growth and survival of new neurons relative to either
  intervention alone — the additive effect that motivates his own research programme.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- On the human analogue he points to a randomised controlled trial comparing orienteering,
  hiking and control groups, in which both active groups beat the control on memory and
  executive function but the orienteering group — combining endurance movement with
  spatial navigation — gained an extra boost; his own lab built a game playable on an
  exercise machine and found a larger cognitive benefit than exercise or the game alone.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- His practical translation is to make workouts spatially challenging — new routes rather
  than the same two or three — and he notes that treadmill running may resemble a running
  wheel without enrichment, while the "green exercise" research literature suggests
  moving in parks and on trails delivers extra mood benefit over indoor or urban
  settings.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- From his fieldwork with the Hadza of northern Tanzania, begun in 2009, he reports step
  counts of 15,000–20,000 a day, with adults in their 70s and early 80s still logging
  60–80 minutes daily of moderate-to-vigorous activity — against a US average for older
  adults of two to four minutes a day.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- He notes the Hadza meet the weekly 150-minute guideline in about two days, whereas only
  25% of US adults meet it at all — a figure he says has not budged in roughly 20 years
  despite decades of public health messaging.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- Biomarker work on the Hadza shows little evidence of cardiovascular disease, diabetes or
  dementia, which he corroborates with the longer-running study of the Tsimane in South
  America; his conclusion is that these are not inevitable features of ageing.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- The most surprising Hadza finding, he says, is that they rest as much as Westerners do —
  but in squatting, kneeling and ground-sitting postures that require low-level muscle
  activity, whereas chairs (in the archaeological record only about 5,000 years old)
  remove the load entirely, halting the triglyceride breakdown that fuels muscle and
  letting those fuels accumulate in the bloodstream.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- People now sit about 9 to 10 hours a day on average, and his prescription is not
  standing all day but breaking sitting up — "exercise snacks" every 30–45 minutes, stairs,
  push-ups, squats, or simply drinking more water so bathroom trips force interruptions.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- He supports this with epidemiological work on vigorous intermittent physical activity —
  brief, non-purposeful bursts like a minute or two of brisk stair-climbing — associated
  with lower mortality and cardiovascular risk independent of deliberate exercise, plus
  lab trials showing that interrupting eight hours of sitting with two-minute treadmill
  walks changes insulin and triglyceride markers.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- On alcohol he says consumption above roughly one drink a day for women or one to two for
  men is associated with negative outcomes including dementia risk; on sleep he describes a
  U-shaped curve with a sweet spot around seven to nine hours, cautioning that
  oversleeping may be a marker for depression rather than a cause of harm, and noting sleep
  is when the brain appears to clear the plaques linked to neurodegeneration.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- He names diet, physical activity and social connection as the three pillars of healthy
  ageing, says the quality of connections matters more than the number — whether you have
  people you can confide in — and describes the post-pandemic loneliness epidemic as
  frightening.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- On air pollution he reports that people in higher-pollution areas face greater dementia
  risk, and that his own work using UK data found that being active in even moderately
  polluted air diminishes — though does not reverse — the brain benefits of that activity.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- His earlier work on the endocannabinoid system found exercise upregulates the body's own
  cannabis-like signalling, which he suggests partly explains why movement feels good —
  and, in a study he calls a favourite, that dogs get the same effect.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
- On diet he says there is no magic pill and the strongest evidence favours Mediterranean-
  style eating — more plant-based, more legumes and whole grains, less processed food, less
  meat, less sugar — while acknowledging the field depends on self-reported intake because
  decade-long dietary trials are unworkable.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]

# Takeaways

Raichlen's advice is deliberately modest: find the activity you will actually keep doing
rather than the theoretically optimal one, start small enough that the first steps feel
achievable, and stay with it long enough for the rewarding neurochemistry to kick in,
since beginners who overshoot the intensity may miss the mild feel-good effect that
sustains the habit.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
He is candid that adherence data has not improved and that ageing populations worldwide
are trending toward US and UK patterns, which is why he now sees reducing sitting as the
more promising place to move the needle.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
Asked how his work can help the world, he answers that sitting less and moving more helps
not only the individual but families, caregivers and economies — pointing to projections
that the US could spend a trillion dollars a year on Alzheimer's by 2050, and arguing that
with no drugs likely to make a large dent, prevention is the biggest available lever.[^the-brain-doctor-5-popular-habits-that-will-kill-WLWbFVKFffM]
