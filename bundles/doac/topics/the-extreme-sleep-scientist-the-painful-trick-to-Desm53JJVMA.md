# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Guy Leschziner, a consultant neurologist and sleep physician who ran the Sleep Disorders Centre at Guy's and St Thomas' Hospital from 2013 to 2023 — one of Europe's largest, with 10 inpatient beds, around 2,500 sleep studies a year and, on his estimate, upwards of 100,000 patients seen over his career.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA] Leschziner's stated interest is people at the extremes of human experience — those who sleepwalk, act out dreams, cannot feel pain or taste words — and what those outliers reveal about ordinary brains. He is unusually cautious for a sleep expert, repeatedly refusing to prescribe a single ideal night: genetics set both chronotype and sleep need, a handful of families he has met sleep four hours with no apparent ill effect, and the population-level 7 to 8.5 hour window says little about any individual.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]

The episode's central practical claim is that insomnia is a treatable psychological and behavioural condition rather than a chemical deficit, and that the fix is counter-intuitive: make people sleepier. Leschziner explains sleep restriction within CBT-I — compressing time in bed to match actual sleep so the homeostatic drive rebuilds the Pavlovian association between bed and sleep — and describes the Australian "intensive sleep retraining" protocol, in which patients already awake 36 hours are given 50 chances to nap over 25 hours and woken after three minutes each time, a technique he calls effective but "basically a form of torture."[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA] Running through this is his insistence that "we are really poor witnesses to our own sleep": patients who report two or three hours routinely show seven or eight on EEG, and the complaint is often about experience rather than duration.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]

That scepticism extends to the sleep-optimisation industry and, pointedly, to sleep experts on podcasts. Leschziner argues trackers help the person who is simply burning the candle at both ends but harm the person already anxious about sleep, since there is no equivalent of "get up and walk 5,000 more steps," and he says he has seen people spiral into insomnia and severe depression after reading books about how vital sleep is.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA] The conversation's final third moves to his wider neurological work — sleep as a state the brain can enter piecemeal, sleepwalking crimes, synaesthesia, congenital insensitivity to pain — all in service of the argument that behaviour and even perceived reality follow from brain function.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]

# Key points

- The prevalence figures he gives: roughly 20% of adults are chronically sleep deprived, 30% will have a period of insomnia in any year, 10% have chronic insomnia (over three months), 10-12% of adult men and about 6% of women have clinically significant obstructive sleep apnoea, restless leg syndrome affects around 5%, and sleepwalking 1-2%; about 80% of UK sleep apnoea goes undiagnosed.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- He distinguishes chronic sleep deprivation (not enough time in bed, a lifestyle problem) from insomnia (wanting to sleep and being unable to), and notes insomnia appears rare in pre-industrial societies studied by researchers such as Jerome Siegel — some tribes lack a word for it.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- On duration: population data show harms below 7 and above 8.5 hours, but he argues the long-sleep end is confounded — medication, undiagnosed sleep disorders, and prodromal brain disease, noting that people with Parkinson's often act out dreams up to three decades before diagnosis, and that sleep change may be an early Alzheimer's feature rather than a cause.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- Sleep loss and weight: hormonal changes in appetite and satiety mean a single night of deprivation can sharply raise calorie intake, glucose tolerance and insulin resistance worsen, and an 18-year nurses' cohort showed those sleeping under six hours started heavier and gained substantially more; treating sleep apnoea with CPAP has let some patients lose weight they previously could not.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- On circadian biology: roughly 40% of genes in a single cell show a 24-hour cycle, the suprachiasmatic nucleus in the hypothalamus is the master clock, and specialised blue-light-sensitive retinal ganglion cells — not conscious vision — set it; he cites one study finding 40% of totally blind individuals have a non-24-hour rhythm, and dismisses the old claim about light behind the knees.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- He revises the blue-light-from-phones story: the light output is probably too weak to cause insomnia directly, and the real damage is a delayed circadian phase from habitual late use plus the arousing content itself, which also erodes the bed-sleep association.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- Twin studies put up to 50% of chronotype under genetic control, and chronotype shifts with age — teenagers drift later, older adults earlier; he adds that reduced sleep need in old age is modest and mostly reflects weaker sleep-stabilising mechanisms.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- On the glymphatic system: research around 2011 suggested brain drainage channels open roughly 60% in deep sleep, clearing metabolites including beta-amyloid, but he flags that a study published only weeks before the interview contradicted the 60% figure, and he declines to claim the sleep-Alzheimer's link is causally settled — noting sleeping tablets have themselves been linked to dementia.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- He describes "local sleep": islands of cortex dipping in and out of electrical silence even while awake, spreading and lengthening with deprivation — likely one reason cognition declines — and the same principle explains sleepwalking, where frontal lobes and hippocampus stay asleep while motor, visual and emotional regions show waking activity.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- On forensic sleep cases he recounts Kenneth Parks, who drove 23 km in Ontario, bludgeoned his mother-in-law to death with a tyre iron and attacked his father-in-law, and was acquitted as a sleepwalker; Leschziner has personally seen patients drive cars and ride a motorbike asleep, and has been involved in cases of shooting and sexual assault, while stressing courts weigh prior behaviour, evidence of planning or cover-up, and sleep-lab findings.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- His treatment hierarchy puts CBT-I first, which he says helps up to about 80% of people; sleeping tablets are a case-by-case judgement when non-drug routes fail and the functional cost is severe; melatonin is broadly safe but invites the question of why it is needed, and can act as a psychological crutch; magnesium he reports as anecdotally helpful, particularly in restless legs.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- Bartlett raises "sleep identity" — the story people tell about being a bad sleeper — and Leschziner agrees it is fundamental, reinforced by trackers whose accuracy for night-time awakenings and sleep staging he says degrades badly even where total time in bed is reasonable.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
- His non-sleep cases make the same point about brains defining behaviour and reality: a man for whom every word carries a taste (synaesthesia, present in some form in about 4% of people and more common in autism), Bill Oddie's musical hallucinations arising from hearing loss, a blind woman who declined treatment for her hallucinations because seeing something felt better than nothing, an elderly patient made so aggressive by an anti-epileptic drug that six officers restrained her — she normalised when it was switched — and a young man born unable to feel pain who held his hands to a fire and jumped off a garage roof.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]

# Takeaways

Leschziner's overall register is deflationary: sleep matters enormously, but he thinks the public conversation has swung from "sleep is for wimps" to an obsessive perfectionism that itself manufactures insomnia, and he includes his own profession's podcast and book appearances in that indictment.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA] His counsel to poor sleepers is to identify what is actually wrong rather than assume insomnia, to expect treatment to take patience rather than deliver an instant chemical fix, to put the tracker away if it is feeding anxiety, and to take the odds seriously — with CBT-I helping around four in five, "the odds are in your favour."[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA] The larger thread he draws from his extreme cases is that free will is thinner than we assume: if a tumour, a stroke or a single tablet can rewrite behaviour, he argues, such behaviours may be better examined biologically than morally.[^the-extreme-sleep-scientist-the-painful-trick-to-Desm53JJVMA]
