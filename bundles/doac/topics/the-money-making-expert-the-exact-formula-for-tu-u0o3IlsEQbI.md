# Overview

Entrepreneur and *Key Person of Influence* author Daniel Priestley tells host Steven Bartlett that he has started seven businesses that went from zero to a million in revenue in their first twelve months and three that passed ten million, and that roughly 4,500 companies have been through the accelerator he founded twelve years ago[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI]. His framing for the episode is generational: where the baby boomers got access to affordable housing, he argues this generation gets access to affordable global small businesses, and the entrepreneurial journey is far more process-driven and predictable than most people assume[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].

The core method he describes is testing demand before building supply. Priestley's repeated instruction is to "sharpen our ideas in the market, not in our minds" by running fast, cheap experiments — waiting-list landing pages, WhatsApp discussion groups and online readiness scorecards — that generate leads and data before a single product exists[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI]. Alongside this he offers a set of mental models: three modes of mind (reptile, autopilot, visionary); the CAPSTONE structure for a pitch; "with or without you" energy as the signature of a compelling ask; and functionality versus vitality as the distinction that he says will separate replaceable work from irreplaceable work in an AI economy[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].

The second half turns to money and scale. Priestley lays out a staged journey — concept, audience, offer, sales, then team building, digitisation of assets and the hard jump from twelve to thirty people — and argues that his own investing thesis is deliberately boring (S&P 500) because he would rather create something investable than pick investments[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI]. His largest claim is about acquisition: with what he says is 65% of business equity in the economy owned by baby boomers, buying a declining "boring boomer business" on vendor finance is, in his view, a bigger opportunity than starting from a blank page[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].

# Key points

- Priestley's first move for any idea is a waiting-list landing page, which he calls the most powerful early-stage experiment available; he cites Elon Musk's waiting lists for the Model 3, the Cybertruck and a flamethrower, and argues that a million people putting down $100 deposits is what made a Cybertruck factory financeable when no investment bank would have backed the design on its own[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- He gives his own worked example: one LinkedIn post about an AI book-writing tool drew 750 waiting-list signups against an expectation of 150, and the survey questions attached to the list (what would you pay per month, what would success and failure look like, what would you use instead) let him spec the product and raise £300,000 from angel investors under the SEIS scheme at a £3 million valuation with no code written[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- Priestley redefines passion as an alignment of origin, mission and vision rather than liking a hobby — he wants a founder's story to go back to age ten, as his own does to a garage sale he ran after a house fire — and says the misaligned version ("I heard someone pumped a crypto coin, I just want to be rich") fails because it repels the talented people a founder needs[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- He describes three modes of mind: reptile (fight, flight, freeze, lashing out at the people trying to help), autopilot (repeat what worked last week), and visionary (long time-frames, the world as one small place, resources a couple of conversations away); he cites research on Indian farmers paid annually who scored roughly 15 IQ points lower at the end of the cycle than just after the lump sum, arguing that financial scarcity mechanically degrades thinking[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- His pitch structure spells CAPSTONE — clarity, authority, problem, solution, the why, opportunity, next steps, essence — and he insists on finishing on essence rather than logistics, because pitches that end on a to-do list "land flat" while people remember how you made them feel[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- The best asks, he says, carry "with or without you" energy — the thing is happening whether you join or not — and he illustrates it with his arrival in London with a suitcase and a credit card: a roughly £1,500 dinner party for 30 industry figures, at which he booked 28 one-to-one meetings, produced launch support from a database of 600,000 people and around £4 million of sales in the first few months[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- Bartlett offers his own examples of asking — five emails at sixteen that produced a vending-machine deal for his school, and twenty emails at eighteen that got Samsung to send £10,000 of camera equipment within 72 hours — and Priestley matches them with under-18s nightclub nights he cold-called into existence at eighteen, taking £10,000 in a night from a thousand people paying £10 a head[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- On why waiting lists convert, Priestley argues buying requires near-100% certainty while joining a list requires only 5-10%, then creates an uncertainty gap the business can warm up; Bartlett reports a portfolio company whose click-through rose 500% and conversion roughly 300% purely from changing a button from "become a member now" to "join the waiting list", and Priestley adds Glastonbury's 700,000 registrations for 140,000 tickets and Rolex's shift to selling the waiting list rather than the watch[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- He dismisses idea theft — "until you've got a Ferrari, no one steals your idea" — on the grounds that ideas are worthless and value accrues to whoever executes; his corollary is that early-stage founders should stop calling themselves entrepreneurs and start calling themselves salespeople[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- Priestley's central distinction is functionality versus vitality: the school system was built to produce reliable components that must not fail, but in an AI world value swings to the irreplaceable life force a person breathes into a project — his example being two magicians using the identical fake thumb, where only one makes you believe[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- He argues environment dictates performance, drawing on prison work with the charity Keys for Life where young men with entrepreneurial instincts sold drugs because the only successful entrepreneur they had met was a dealer; his prescription is to change environment for an hour or two a week, and he names the resulting friction with old friends "social shedding"[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- On team structure he copies the British military's 2-4-8-30 progression (scout team, fire team, section, platoon), staffing an idea with two people, a proven one with four, a running one with eight and a standalone business with thirty; he also screens partners by the four card suits — clubs for vision, spades for execution, hearts for connection, diamonds for money and data — and looks for all four energies in one team[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- His staged growth map runs concept, audience, offer, sales — which he says should reach six figures of revenue on its own — measured weekly through a "rhythm of LAPS" (leads, appointments, presentations, sales); then a key person of influence plus a core team of eight, then digitising every asset to raise revenue per person before adding headcount, with the jump from twelve to thirty people the hardest because a team of rebels and misfits must become a professional organisation and some early hires have to go[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- Asked what to do with £100 a month, Priestley says small-sum investing will not change a life and the money is better spent on skills courses or, his personal preference, taking one accomplished-but-not-famous person to dinner each month; his stated first principle is that income follows assets, and his own portfolio is "extremely boring" — S&P 500 — because he considers great investors pessimists and himself an optimistic business creator[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- The acquisition opportunity he calls the "boring boomer business": with 65% of business equity owned by baby boomers, he describes buying a retiring owner's company with no money down by paying out over six or seven years, securing the loan against the business itself and making the seller board chair — citing a friend, Kit King, who spent about £25,000 digitising a paper-spike order system, re-engaged a dozen disengaged staff and grew the business roughly 500% in two years[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- On AI, Priestley says he had a "we've hit an iceberg" moment, that humans supply context while AI supplies content, and that Sam Altman's bet on a one-person billion-dollar company is directionally right but will be beaten by five- or ten-person AI teams; his warning is that AI will push people to the edges of either consumption or creation, and everyone will have to choose which[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
- He rejects work-life balance as a category error, noting that people earning over £100,000 typically work around 55 hours a week, and offers the test he considers decisive: hard work that simultaneously builds an asset — equity, a podcast back catalogue, a renovated property, reputation — compounds, while hard work that builds none, such as driving for Uber, eventually turns toxic[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].

# Takeaways

Priestley's argument is that entrepreneurship only looks like a gamble because most people commit before they test — booking offices, taking qualifications and building supply for demand that was never verified[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI]. His alternative is a sequence anyone can run for almost nothing: launch a waiting list, ask the joiners questions, open a discussion group, run a scorecard, and let the market rather than the mind decide which idea survives[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI]. Around that he layers a view of value in which the scarce thing is not functionality — which he expects AI to absorb — but the vitality, pitching ability and network of relationships that get a thing made at all, and he closes by pointing at the boring, declining, baby-boomer-owned businesses he thinks ambitious people are walking past in favour of startups from scratch[^the-money-making-expert-the-exact-formula-for-tu-u0o3IlsEQbI].
