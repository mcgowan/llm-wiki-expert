# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Ramani Durvasula, a licensed clinical psychologist with a PhD in clinical psychology and a minor in health psychology, and the author of books including *Should I Stay or Should I Go?*, *Don't You Know Who I Am?* and *It's Not You*.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] She argues that exposure to narcissistic people shapes — or in adulthood hijacks — a person's sense of identity, and that mental health as a field has been reluctant to name the problem, as though it were impolite to say that narcissistic people are not very nice.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] She says she still sees clients every week and prefers the word "survivor" to "victim," since the people affected were never taught what they were dealing with.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]

Her working definition goes well beyond arrogance: variable and typically low empathy, deep entitlement, an excessive need for admiration and validation, emotional shallowness, an inability to sustain deep intimacy, and goals set entirely by reference to the outside world.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] Behaviourally it appears as devaluation, dismissiveness, manipulation, gaslighting, rapid anger, betrayal, blame-shifting and a refusal to take responsibility — with the distinguishing feature from an ordinary bad day being that non-narcissistic people take accountability, make amends and change.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] The comparison she draws is with addiction: partners of addicts get social recognition of their situation, whereas partners of narcissists are told how lucky they are, and so blame themselves.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]

The conversation covers the four subtypes, developmental origins, prevalence, workplace and political narcissism, gaslighting and DARVO, and what she calls radical acceptance. Durvasula is emphatic that narcissism cannot be cured because it would mean changing a personality, but equally emphatic that the people harmed by it recover routinely, and closes by disclosing her own experience of being stalked and sexually assaulted at university.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]

# Key points

- On prevalence, Durvasula notes that large epidemiological studies put narcissistic personality *disorder* at somewhere between 1 and 6%, but says that narcissism as a noticeable personality style — enough that others experience it — is her "spitball number" of roughly 15–18%, or about one in six, and probably higher in major cities and certain industries.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- She distinguishes four subtypes: grandiose (showy, charismatic, often successful, vindictive when let down); vulnerable (socially anxious, sullen, aggrieved, "failure to launch," which she says is gender-balanced while grandiose and malignant forms skew male); malignant (manipulative, coercive, menacing — "the last stop on the train before you hit psychopathy station"); and communal.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Communal narcissism, a construct she traces to work by Gebauer around 2003, describes people who extract narcissistic supply from being *seen* as saviours and humanitarians rather than from overt self-promotion — at the mild end "Instagram saviours" cleaning up the beach in a bikini, at the severe end the cult leader, which she says is where communal and malignant narcissism meet.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- She separates malignant narcissism from psychopathy, arguing malignant narcissists know they did wrong while psychopaths have literally zero capacity for empathy, and references the dark tetrad — narcissism, psychopathy, Machiavellianism and sadism — adding that she would personally add paranoia as a fifth element.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- On causes she describes two pathways layered on innate temperament: a more biologically vulnerable, hard-to-soothe temperament meeting an invalidating environment of trauma, neglect, chaos or emotional abuse; and the "overvalued child" pathway associated with Eddie Brummelman at the University of Amsterdam and earlier writers like Masterson, in which a child is told they are more special than everyone else, never learns to self-soothe, and is materially indulged while remaining emotionally undernourished.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Because most children on either pathway do not become narcissistic, she calls narcissism "one of those stories we can always tell backwards, but it's really difficult to tell forwards," and refuses to diagnose adolescents — telling worried parents to call her in ten years.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- She rejects the idea that social media creates narcissists, calling it "a narcissist amplifier, but not a creator" — an accelerant on a fire already burning, since narcissists previously had to leave the house to obtain validation; her larger concern is that it has inflamed vulnerable narcissism, which she offers as much of the explanation for internet trolling, and that it harms non-narcissistic people who already feel they don't measure up.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Asked whether narcissists are more professionally successful, she answers yes without hesitation: success is validation and therefore "the air in their lungs," so they are over-represented in leadership, earn more than agreeable men and do better in dating — and she argues narcissism and capitalism fit together because outcome-heavy metrics that ignore process always favour them.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- On whether great innovation justifies the personality, she concedes that many wartime leaders and inventors were probably narcissistic and that narcissistic people are "built for innovation," calls the empathic CEO "a unicorn" because genuine caring in that role would cause burnout, and lands on her own summary: "We have gotten lots of cool stuff in our lives from them. Just don't marry them."[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Money, she argues, does not create narcissism but produces "feet that never touch the ground" entitlement, citing Paul Piff's research in which people made to feel wealthier endorsed more unethical behaviour and gave less to charity, and his finding that drivers of luxury cars were less likely to make a full stop at stop signs; she adds that at least a dozen published studies show narcissistic people drive more dangerously.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Narcissists rarely recognise themselves, she says, because research shows they overestimate their empathy and underestimate their negative effect on others; the rare "self-aware narcissistic people" she has met treat it as a superpower — "don't hate the player, hate the game" — and she warns that many people who ask if they are narcissists have simply been gaslit into it by a narcissistic partner.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Narcissistic relationships, she says, start strong — "running their fastest miles in the beginning of the marathon" — with the devaluation phase typically beginning somewhere between six weeks and six months; the people who get stuck are the more empathic, forgiving and optimistic, and she calls the process "death by a thousand cuts."[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- She names three Rs that characterise being in such a relationship: rumination (obsessively trying to make sense of something that makes no sense), regret (which shades into grief about the parent, marriage or decades lost) and euphoric recall (cherry-picking rare good moments — her example being a partner who once brought home a blueberry muffin — to justify staying), which together produce a trauma-bonded relationship.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Her account of the internal machinery is a volcano of shame and insecurity covered by a manhole cover of grandiosity, arrogance and charm; any frustration — criticism, a friend doing better, a failed venture, a wrong coffee order — nudges the cover and the lava escapes as rage and projection, which she says happens perhaps twenty times a day, and which is why they cannot absorb feedback except as compliance toward someone more powerful, then go home and rage at a safer target.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- Gaslighting she defines as a power play that requires a presumption of trust, escalating from denying your memories to attacking your character when you produce evidence — because "this isn't about evidence, this is about them overpowering you" — and she describes DARVO (deny, attack, reverse victim and offender), a construct from Dr Jennifer Freyd, as the tactic that shuts down accountability; her advice is to disengage entirely rather than produce texts or photos, with a line like "we're having a different experience."[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- She states as a personal and admittedly controversial belief that all domestic abusers are narcissistic without exception, on the grounds that assaulting someone you claim to love requires zero empathy, enormous entitlement and arrogance — which she offers as an explanation for why domestic violence intervention programmes so often fail — while insisting narcissism never excuses the behaviour.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- For narcissistic bosses her practical advice is to document everything, since HR cannot act on a diagnosis, to avoid one-on-one meetings, and to consider moving within a large organisation; she notes research suggesting teams sometimes bond more strongly under narcissistic leadership, united against a common enemy.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- On public life she estimates 40–50% of famous people and 60–70% of world leaders are narcissistic, arguing the quest for fame is itself narcissistically driven and that a non-narcissist could not win at modern politics; she compares voters to magic show audiences who prefer the illusion to the explanation, and wishes diplomats and secretaries of state received training in narcissism, since negotiating with a narcissistic world leader is as futile as negotiating with a narcissistic spouse.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
- The narcissist's kryptonite, in her framing, is disengagement — refusing the fight, refusing to ooh and aah — and authenticity, which they will mock and try to dismantle but eventually find boring enough to walk away from.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]

# Takeaways

Durvasula's central claim is that narcissism cannot be cured — she has seen micro-changes in therapy but never a narcissist become a non-narcissist — and that this makes "radical acceptance" the key step for anyone who stays: accepting that there is no someday better, grieving the hope that sustained them, and then building a life of healthy connection around the relationship rather than inside it.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] She is explicit that she is not telling people to leave, since financial, cultural, familial and parental reasons may make leaving impossible, but warns that "staying and not radically accepting it will destroy you."[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo] Her closing insistence is that the episode should not read as fatalism: with education, validation of their experience and permission to name abusive behaviour as abuse, survivors of familial, marital and workplace narcissistic abuse recover routinely and often emerge wiser and more creative — it is work, but it is not destiny.[^the-narcissism-doctor-1-in-6-people-are-narcissi-hTkKXDvSJvo]
