# Overview

Therapist and trainer Marisa Peer — whose clients have included royalty, international CEOs and Olympic athletes, and who says she has trained 16,000 therapists — returns to the show to argue that beliefs are chosen, not inherited, and that the job of the mind is to make your thoughts real[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. Her opening claim to host Steven Bartlett is that you should "lie, cheat and steal every day" — lie to your mind, cheat fear, and steal back the confidence you were born with — because the subconscious does not think, it only feels, and the mind learns by repetition[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. She demonstrates this live with an imagined lemon that produces real saliva and an arm-swing that reaches a third further after a single instruction.

Much of the conversation applies that framework to sex and relationships. Peer distinguishes intimacy — knowing someone completely — from eroticism, which she says requires mystery, suspense and edginess, and argues the two do not naturally coexist; fantasy is the bridge between them[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. She warns that couples who let each other become "mummy" or "daddy" destroy desire, that pornography sets impossible expectations, and that erectile problems are frequently a thought problem that responds to changing the thought[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].

The second half becomes unusually personal. Bartlett walks through his own pattern of self-sabotage between 20 and 25, and Peer traces it to a childhood belief formed as a Black kid in a white area with a house he never brought anyone home to[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. She then hypnotises him on air to address a recurring sugar spiral, surfacing a memory of a disappointing school lunchbox; in an insert recorded two weeks later, Bartlett reports no sugar cravings at all, weight loss and improved sleep[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].

# Key points

- Peer's model of belief is that you make your beliefs and then they make you, with confirmation bias supplying the evidence; her instruction is to constantly audit them — where did I get that, who told me, and even if it is true for them must it be true for me — offering the example that a grandmother's belief that rich successful women cannot find a partner has no claim on a granddaughter[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Her stated rules of the mind are that every thought is a blueprint the body works to make real, that the mind learns by repetition, and that whatever you look for you will find; she illustrates fear shutting cognition down with her own memory of being followed home and being unable to remember how her own key worked after five years in the flat[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- She runs two live demonstrations: a guided imaginary lemon that makes Bartlett salivate — "was there a lemon?" — and an arm swing that travels a third further simply after telling the arm it will, which she offers as proof that instruction alone changes physical performance[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Applying this to erectile difficulty, Peer says men who repeat "I can't keep it going, she'll leave me" make that real, and that clients who instead listen to recordings telling them they are a good lover with lasting erections improve without doing anything else; she notes arousal from a picture alone proves thoughts produce physical response with no one in the room[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- On pressure she and Bartlett agree comparison is the thief of joy, with porn creating an impossible standard and, she says, "every year there's another way to hate your body" now extending even to genitals[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Her core relationship distinction is intimacy versus eroticism: intimacy is being loved regardless of periods, bad breath or tiredness; eroticism wants mystery, suspense, naughtiness and the unknown; the two are in tension, and many affairs happen not because people stopped loving their partner but because they feel they are missing out[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Peer's most concrete claim concerns fertility: citing the book *Sperm Wars*, she says men having sex with a stranger triple their sperm output and women's cervix tilts to draw up sperm, and reports instructing couples with low sperm counts to act out a wordless stranger fantasy in a hotel — with several conceiving as a result[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- She warns that calling a partner "mummy" or "daddy", or drifting into critical, controlling or caretaking parent roles ("have you taken your vitamins", "did you pay the bill"), kills desire, because nobody wants to have sex with their parent — a slide she says accelerates once children arrive[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- On requests, she recommends the sandwich approach — affirm the relationship, name the fantasy, explain why it would excite you — and argues that doing something for a partner you would not choose yourself is simply what relationships are, provided the fantasy is not dangerous or painful; she is blunt that unilaterally ending a couple's sex life condemns the other person to none[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Peer frames self-sabotage and procrastination as the mind obeying a stated wish: if you tell it commitment is prison, it will find non-logical ways to get you out, and she gives the example of a client who could not tell his wife he had been fired, walked to the park with his briefcase each day, and became so ill he had to resign — "the feeling that cannot find its expression in tears will make other organs weep"[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- She sorts relationship needs into three categories — the non-negotiable ones a partner must meet (for her, honesty and knowing where he is), the ones you can meet yourself, and the ones to give up entirely — using her artist daughter's messy bedroom as the case for choosing a happy daughter over a tidy room[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Asked about a woman in her thirties who cannot find a partner, Peer's answer is that she is not actually looking: she goes to yoga classes and bars full of other women, and should instead decide what qualities she wants, work out where that person actually is, and first establish that she deserves love by repeating it until it stops feeling stupid; she tells a man to leave the weight room for yoga and a woman the reverse[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- On dating apps she argues excessive choice works like a twenty-page menu, and that seeing fifty conventionally beautiful people first destroys your ability to value the people who actually match with you, since value is judged by context[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Peer says the three things wrong with everyone who walks through a therapist's door are "I'm different so I can't connect", "I want something that isn't available to me", and "I'm not enough" — and maps all three onto Bartlett's childhood; her prescription is not logic but repetition, since "in a battle between emotion and logic, emotion wins every single time"[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- The on-air hypnosis frames cravings as a search for a feeling rather than a substance: Peer's "magic sentence" is "I'm choosing to say no and I'm choosing to love it", on the logic that prohibition creates wanting; she surfaces Bartlett's memory of a disappointing lunchbox at seven and the powerlessness he calls learned helplessness, and has him repeat that it is not him anymore[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
- Bartlett's two-week follow-up insert reports no sugar cravings whatsoever despite being around chocolate, weight loss and improved sleep, and he links the full 25-minute hypnosis session on the show's clips channel[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].

# Takeaways

Peer's through-line is that the feeling mind cannot be argued with, only re-trained — which is why she thinks reading books about confidence or "faking it till you make it" fails, since people detect low self-worth through a thousand signals below the level of communication[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. Bartlett's own account supports her framing: he says nothing changed for him until his actual opinion of himself changed, at which point the pattern of rejection stopped[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE]. Her closing answer to the question of the single sentence everyone should believe is "I am enough", which she has taken into schools through the I Am Enough movement, on the grounds that the alternative — believing you are not enough — is what drives the search for more chocolate, more followers, more drinks and more shopping[^the-no-1-celebrity-therapist-the-weird-trick-to-_y_vSmiNpBE].
