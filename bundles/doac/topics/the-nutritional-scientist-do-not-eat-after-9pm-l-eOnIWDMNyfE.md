# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Sarah Berry, a professor of nutrition science at King's College London and chief scientist at Zoe, who has spent about 25 years running randomized controlled trials on how diet affects cardiometabolic health. Her central argument is that nutrition science must move beyond back-of-pack nutrient labels: two foods with identical labels can have entirely different health effects depending on the "food matrix" — the physical structure of the food — and on how, when, and why we eat it. She frames health response to food as a puzzle combining who you are (genetics, microbiome, age, sex, menopause status), the food's structure and roughly 70,000 chemicals, and how you eat (sleep, stress, timing, eating rate).[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]

Berry argues that processing is a double-edged sword rather than inherently evil: it makes food safe, stable, and edible, but goes wrong when it strips fiber and bioactives, adds saturated fat, salt, and sugar, and makes food so soft and energy-dense that we eat it faster than our fullness signals can catch up. She illustrates this with her own almond and oat trials, the 1977 Haber apple-versus-puree study, and her snack-swap trial in which replacing typical UK snacks with almonds for six weeks improved vascular function by an amount equating to a 30% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk. She also stresses chrononutrition: her group found snacking after 9pm was associated with worse belly fat, inflammation, and blood lipids even when the snacks were healthy.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]

A large portion of the conversation is devoted to what Berry calls "nutribollocks" — nutrition misinformation. She argues there is no credible evidence that seed oils are harmful (calling out RFK Jr's beef-tallow advocacy), that fermented dairy like cheese and yogurt does not raise cholesterol the way butter does, that nuts do not cause weight gain, and that dietary cholesterol from foods like eggs barely affects circulating cholesterol at normal intakes. She closes with research on menopause — where she says a healthier diet was associated with roughly a 35% reduction in symptoms — and a plea for balance: food should bring pleasure, and "if a food is too healthy to be enjoyed, it's just not healthy at all."[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]

# Key points

- Berry argues the "food matrix" — the physical structure encapsulating a food's nutrients and ~70,000 chemicals — modulates its health effects, so two foods with identical labels and calories can affect the body completely differently depending on processing.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- Citing Professor Ciarán Forde's research, she says slowing eating speed by about 20% cuts calorie intake by roughly 15%; in Zoe's PREDICT data, fast eaters consume about 120 more calories per day than slow eaters, and trials show people told to eat slower lose more weight. Heavily processed soft-textured foods can be eaten about 50% faster than harder unprocessed equivalents.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- She cites evidence that chewing food 40 times versus 15 times changes how full a meal makes you feel, and notes fullness hormones take roughly 10-20 minutes to fully kick in — long enough to finish a tube of crisps before your brain registers eating.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- She describes Haber's 1977 Lancet study: identical calories as whole apples versus apple puree — the puree was consumed three to four times faster, left people less full, and caused a blood-sugar dip; Zoe research finds "dippers" eat about 180 more calories at their next meal.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- In her almond work, intact cell walls mean about 20-30% of a whole nut's calories are excreted rather than absorbed (a labeled 170-calorie portion yields roughly 130), while feeding the gut microbiome; grinding the nut makes the fat 100% bioaccessible. Her oat trials found finely ground oats produce about a 50% higher post-meal glucose response than large steel-cut oats.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- Her six-week snack-swap RCT (funded by the Almond Board of California, a conflict she declares) replaced 20% of energy from typical UK snacks with almonds; the improvement in flow-mediated dilation equated to a 30% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk. About 25% of UK/US energy comes from snacks, roughly 75% of it from unhealthy ones.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- In a study of 1,000 individuals her team found eating frequency (grazing versus three meals) did not harm health outcomes as long as the food was healthy — but 30% of people snacked after 9pm, which was associated with worse visceral belly fat, higher inflammation, and worse blood lipids even with healthy snacks, consistent with chrononutrition findings that every cell has a body clock.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- Zoe's "Big IF" study enrolled 150,000 people in a 10-hour eating window; participants reported better energy and mood within two weeks, and time-restricted eating typically produces an unintentional ~300-calorie daily reduction — she says most of its benefit is from eating less, with a smaller calorie-independent metabolic benefit. She also cites emerging evidence favoring consistent day-to-day eating and sleeping patterns; "social jet lag" (sleep timing shifting more than about 1.5 hours) is associated with poorer diet, more inflammation, and altered gut microbiome.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- Sleep interacts with diet: in Zoe research a bad night's sleep raised post-breakfast glucose responses as much as changing the meal's macronutrients, and the King's College "Slumber" study found short sleepers who improved sleep hygiene spontaneously cut free-sugar intake by about 10 grams a day.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- On seed oils, she says there is "absolutely no credible evidence" of harm: a meta-analysis of about 42 RCTs shows seed oils reduce cholesterol and cardiovascular risk, the omega-6 pro-inflammation argument fails in human trials, and the oft-cited 1970s Sydney heart study is confounded because its seed oils were partially hydrogenated and full of trans fats. Beef tallow, which RFK Jr promotes as a replacement, is high in palmitic saturated fat and consistently performed worse in trials.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- Further "nutribollocks" she challenges: fermented dairy like cheese and yogurt does not raise cholesterol despite its saturated fat (unlike butter with near-identical fat composition); nuts do not cause weight gain in trials; one to two eggs a day won't meaningfully raise circulating LDL because liver production — driven by saturated fat and refined carbohydrate — matters far more than dietary cholesterol; and saturated-fat studies must ask "instead of what," since replacing it with whole grains or unsaturated fats helps while swapping to refined carbs shows no difference.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
- On menopause: her research in 70,000 individuals found 99% of perimenopausal women report at least one symptom and 66% report twelve or more; hot flushes affect only about 40% while brain fog and related symptoms affect about 85%; roughly 10% of women leave the workforce over symptoms. Post-menopause, women's LDL cholesterol rises about 25% and heart-attack and abdominal-obesity risk climb about five-fold. Zoe data suggest a healthier, Mediterranean-style diet is associated with roughly a 35% symptom reduction (she caveats there was no control arm, though Mediterranean-diet RCTs show ~30%), soy isoflavones have weak supportive evidence, and she warns against "menowashing" — marketing unproven products to desperate women.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]

# Takeaways

Berry distils the episode into five principles: choose a dietary pattern you enjoy so it is sustainable and consistent; pay attention to how you eat — slow down, chew more, avoid eating late at night, and keep a 10-12 hour eating window; go back to basics with fiber (she notes 95% of people fall short of the ~30g target), whole grains, fruits, vegetables, and healthy oils like olive oil; never think of diet in isolation from sleep, stress, and physical activity; and don't deny yourself anything — think about what to add rather than what to remove. Above all she urges skepticism toward social-media nutrition claims, arguing the boring, caveated voices of research-active scientists are being drowned out by confident misinformation, and that a change disrupting your life for a 1% gain isn't worth the lost pleasure of food.[^the-nutritional-scientist-do-not-eat-after-9pm-l-eOnIWDMNyfE]
