# Overview

Dr Karen Gurney — a clinical psychologist and psychosexologist with 20 years of practice and lead psychologist in an award-winning NHS sex clinic — tells host Steven Bartlett that sex is not a frivolous recreational pursuit but central to psychological and relationship wellbeing, and that as a society "we've got sex all wrong"[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. She cites UK data from 15,000 adults showing 52% of women and 42% of men are unhappy with their sex lives, and says problems with desire remain the single most common reason people arrive in her clinic[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].

Her central correction concerns how desire works. Gurney argues the popular model — a libido that arrives unbidden and tells you to have sex — is a leftover from 1950s and 60s sex science, and that for most people in long-term relationships spontaneous desire reliably declines around twelve to eighteen months in[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. What remains is responsive desire, which emerges out of sexual activity rather than preceding it, and which she says works extremely well — better in women than men — once people stop waiting to feel like it and start deliberately creating the stimuli that trigger it[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].

The practical vehicle for this is her concept of "sexual currency": all the ways a couple relates to each other as sexual beings outside of sex itself — passionate kissing, flirtation, bum grabs, suggestive messages[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. Her prescription is that sex should be "trivial and often, not rare and crucial", that couples should schedule physical intimacy but never sex itself, and that the marker of finished therapy is a couple who find it easy both to invite each other into sex and to turn each other down[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. She applies the same framework to parents, distraction, communication, monogamy — and, in a finding she says surprised her after decades in the job, pets in the bedroom.

# Key points

- Gurney says long-term UK data shows sexual frequency falling decade on decade, and attributes it partly to attention: over 90% of people have non-sexual thoughts during sex, and cognitively salient distractions ("am I going to stay hard", "is she enjoying this") turn arousal down like the volume on a TV, while worry activates the sympathetic nervous system with a chemical message that says do not have sex[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- She notes research showing women who find it easier to orgasm are typically more mindful in everyday life, and points to substantial evidence that mindfulness improves sexual response by sharpening attention — noting that physiological arousal produces attentional narrowing, which is useful unless the attention has already been hijacked by a worry[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Drawing on sexual script theory from 1970s researchers Gagnon and Simon, Gurney argues that in the absence of talking about sex we absorb a set menu from media and porn — penis-in-vagina sex as the "main course", everything else relegated by the word "foreplay", virginity defined only by penetration — which is why couples silently perform a script neither chose[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- She cites analysis of the 100 most popular PornHub videos in which women's pleasure or pleasure-inducing acts for women appeared in around 18% of cases against roughly 78% for men, and connects this directly to the orgasm gap in heterosexual sex[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Gurney says around 80% of women do not orgasm from penetrative sex because pleasure is clitoral, making penis-in-vagina sex women's least favourite act and men's most favourite; she argues this asymmetry damages women's desire, because motivation depends on expecting a reward[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Being able to talk about sex is, she says, more highly correlated with long-term sexual and relationship satisfaction than frequency or even liking the same things sexually; her staged approach is to start with topics outside the relationship (a podcast, a film), then discuss your own sex when it has gone well, and only later move to what you would like more of — never what you dislike[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- On spontaneous versus responsive desire, she reports 34% of women and 15% of men in the UK worry they do not feel like sex enough, and argues it should be considered normal for a woman never to feel out-of-the-blue desire for a long-term partner — the survey question itself only measures one type of desire[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Sexual currency is her name for everything that marks a couple out as sexual apart from sex; her illustration is a man who felt more desired by a ten-second passionate kiss in a lift on the way to a function than by the going-through-the-motions sex they had the week before[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- She is firmly against scheduling sex, which she says creates pressure by asking people to commit in advance to a feeling they cannot predict, but strongly in favour of scheduling physical intimacy — a bath, a massage, getting naked on the sofa — with the explicit caveat that inviting someone into a bath is an invitation to a bath, and reproaching them afterwards destroys the mechanism[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- On initiation, Gurney says the initiator typically reports higher satisfaction, that women initiating with men tend to be very direct ("do you fancy a shag") in a way she calls accurate but inherently unsexy, and that men more often report needing to feel intimacy and genuine enthusiasm rather than a functional offer; she also warns that a typecast initiation style that was cute in month six becomes "here we go again" by month sixty[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Bartlett discloses times he could not get an erection because his to-do list was still in his head, and Gurney names the phenomenon arousal non-concordance — bodies not matching minds, in people of all genders, but visible in those with penises; her advice is that the partner take "I want this, I'm just not there yet" at face value, drop the requirement for a hard penis, and move attention to other sexual activity, adding that the erection usually returns only if nobody makes it the point[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- On frequency she says everyone guesses three times a week, the UK average is roughly three times a month, and — crucially — there is no correlation whatsoever between frequency and sexual satisfaction; over 40% of women want more sex than they currently have[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- For parents, Gurney says sexual satisfaction is at its lowest with an under-five at home, that a good night's sleep raises the chance of sex the next day by about 14%, that the number of night wakings tracks directly with dissatisfaction, and that people typically start sleeping again once the youngest child is around six; she also cites a direct correlation between equitable, transparent division of household labour and sexual satisfaction, warning against the dynamic in which a partner becomes a third child[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- She compares parenthood to sailing through a storm: some phases require battening down the hatches and not worrying about sex, but small course corrections — turning each other down gently, raising sexual currency — compound into an entirely different destination over ten or fifteen years[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- Gurney argues humans are not designed for monogamy — brains want novelty and dislike predictability — and that choosing it is fine so long as people go in eyes open that spontaneous desire and sexual currency will drop and that maintaining interest requires deliberate work; she also flags resentment, and the Gottmans' four horsemen, as her clearest signal that a relationship may be unrecoverable, while noting that a split can itself be a good outcome[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
- The surprise of her recent practice, she says, is pets: a social media post about animals disrupting sex "blew up" with people reporting pets watching, intervening or scratching at the door, a problem she has never seen written up in research and which Bartlett confirms with his own protective dog[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].

# Takeaways

Gurney's diagnosis is that most people are measuring their sex lives against a script no one wrote down and no one checks — three times a week, spontaneous lust, penetration as the main event — and then concluding something is broken in them or their partner[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. Her replacement model is mechanical rather than romantic: desire follows arousal, arousal follows stimuli, stimuli have to be deliberately created, and attention has to be protected from worry and devices for any of it to work[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA]. The advice she leaves Bartlett with condenses the episode — create a culture of talking about sex often, initiate freely, and get genuinely comfortable with being turned down — and she suggests thinking of a psychosexologist less as an emergency service than as a personal trainer for a sex life, someone people should reach far earlier than they do[^the-orgasm-expert-this-is-how-often-you-should-b-E5swtIjpxxA].
