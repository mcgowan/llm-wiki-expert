# Overview

Journalist, podcast host and author Louise Perry — writing from the position she set out in *The Case Against the Sexual Revolution* — tells host Steven Bartlett that the dominant narrative treats the arrival of reliable contraception as unambiguously good with no trade-offs, and that she disagrees on the second half rather than the first[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4]. She grants that the pill lets families be planned and spaces births healthily, but argues its downstream effects on birth rates and sexual culture have been far more consequential than is usually acknowledged, and that the biggest winners have been a small subset of high-status men — the "Hugh Hefners of the world" — rather than men in general[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].

Perry traces her own political movement to working at a rape crisis centre after a progressive university education. Standard feminist theory told her sexual violence was about power, not desire, and that psychological sex differences were negligible; working with victims, she says, she could not help noticing that the modal victim was around 15, that perpetrators skewed young, and that the peak age of male perpetration tracks the testosterone curve[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4]. Her conclusion is that channelling young male aggression is an eternal problem every society faces, and that refusing to state the biological facts leaves young women unprotected[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].

From there the conversation covers what she takes to be the practical consequences: her advice to delay sex, her defence of marriage and monogamy as an equalising institution, the Cinderella effect and stepparents, falling global fertility, pornography, and the normalisation of choking and rough sex among young people[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4]. Throughout she frames herself as saying unpopular things because she believes they are true, and reports that roughly 95% of responses to her book have been positive, most often from worried fathers and from women who lived the culture she describes[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].

# Key points

- Perry says casual sex is "almost always more risky for women than it's worth," citing two asymmetries: being alone with an unknown man is inherently dangerous given the physical gap, and only women bear pregnancy risk — carrying to term or abortion — while the man may never know it occurred; she grounds the same point in reproductive economics, contrasting nine months of pregnancy, risky childbirth and roughly fifteen years of dependency against a man's theoretical capacity to reproduce many times over at minimal cost, which she says explains why women typically want commitment signals rather than marriage per se[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- On psychological sex differences she points to campus propositioning studies where roughly 0% of women accept an attractive stranger's invitation while a high proportion of men do, and argues the cross-cultural consistency is the strongest evidence of something innate — in no country surveyed do women watch more porn, express more desire for casual sex, or buy sex more than men; she frames these as overlapping bell curves rather than categorical differences, with the divergence most visible at the tails, where people who buy sex or become porn-addicted are "basically entirely male"; she extends the same call for honesty to the workplace, suggesting women may outperform in roles such as interviewing and interrogation while objecting to lowered physical standards in policing, firefighting and the military[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- She argues much young women's misery comes from going along with sex they do not want in order to be polite, cool or approved of, connecting this to the intense social sensitivity of teenage girls that she says also makes them the first carriers of socially contagious conditions — Salem, 19th-century hysteria, anorexia, TikTok tics; she adds that women have a lower disgust threshold, and reports that survey data and her own book's quiz show many recall consensual-but-unwanted encounters that now produce a physical disgust response[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Citing evolutionary psychologist David Buss, she describes an eternal tug-of-war in which women prefer to wait and men prefer to accelerate, and says the pill shifted the default from "we will not have sex" to "we might," turning the encounter into a negotiation; she reads the Aziz Ansari case as a product of that ambiguity rather than straightforward criminality, compounded by alcohol and by men's documented bias toward over-reading sexual interest[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Perry endorses Gavin de Becker's *The Gift of Fear*, arguing women's instincts often register threats the conscious mind misses, and cites the finding that women asked to mark streets they would avoid at night map closely onto actual crime rates despite knowing nothing of police statistics — while conceding that social-media-driven "icks" such as height cutoffs make people counterproductively picky[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Her book recommends waiting three months before sex, but she says on the podcast she thinks waiting until engagement is the better call, and admits the three-month figure was pitched partly on her mother's advice that a marriage-or-engagement rule would dominate every review; her stated reasons are that women bond more strongly through sex — she points to oxytocin, and to journalism advising women to take ecstasy to dull the bonding response as evidence the effect is publicly acknowledged — and that a diamond ring is a tested demonstration of commitment against pregnancy risk[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Drawing on her anthropology background, she notes that human universals include marriage customs in every culture, that about 80% of cultures permit polygamous marriage, and that Western societies are unusual in having discarded such conventions entirely — pointing to London, where she says half of children reach 15 without their biological father in the home[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- She describes monogamous marriage as "sexual socialism": female hypergamy on dating apps concentrates attention on high-status men while the bottom tier gets none, and monogamy forces those men to commit and exit the pool; she adds that polygamous societies show more domestic violence and instability, that a surplus of sexless men is destabilising because they have little to lose, and that men's testosterone drops after marriage and children, with new fathers committing less crime[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Challenged on divorce statistics, Perry notes that repeat marriages skew the headline rate, that only about 10% of graduates who marry divorce — suggesting marriage is becoming a luxury good — and that elite graduates "talk the 1960s but walk the 1950s"; she also cites the Cinderella effect, under which children with a stepparent in the home face a hundredfold higher risk of abuse, while stressing this is risk, not a claim about individual stepparents[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- She argues affluence, not feminism or capitalism, is the single factor that best explains collapsing global fertility, with the threshold around $10,000 GDP per capita and sub-Saharan Africa the main remaining exception; she cites mortality salience as a partial mechanism — lab studies show reminding people of death raises desired family size — and notes South Korea's rate near 0.7 implies losing roughly 95% of its population within a century[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- Perry calls pornography a net negative and says she would ban it, objecting both to production conditions — high suicide and drug dependency rates among performers, and a study she attributes to Cunningham finding female porn performers 18 times more likely to be murdered — and to consumption effects, including "death grip syndrome" and what she calls "cultural death grip syndrome," where hyper-abundant sexual stimuli reduce the drive to form relationships and, historically, the drive to acquire a job and a home[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
- She links porn to a normalised violent sexual script, citing charity estimates that 2 million UK women have experienced unwanted choking or strangulation during sex and 3.5 million unwanted slapping, spitting or gagging, alongside a roughly tenfold increase in rough sex between 1996 and 2016 and the "rough sex" defence used in killings; on the sex industry generally she favours the Nordic model of criminalising buyers but not sellers, and argues the voices heard from within it are unrepresentative because people speak while still in it, comparing the self-protective narrative to that of someone inside an abusive relationship[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].

# Takeaways

Perry's argument is that human reproduction is a delicate coordination problem every culture solves with rules, that the 1960s dismantled the Western version of those rules on the assumption that constraint itself was the problem, and that the resulting free-for-all distributes its costs unevenly — onto young women navigating an ambiguous sexual script, and onto the low-status men who get no access at all[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4]. She also presses a second theme: that the cultural devaluation of motherhood is itself sexist, and that a great deal of her writing is an attempt to elevate feminine goods — monogamy, children, caring more about a family than a career — that she says have been needlessly marginalised[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4]. Her defence of saying all this out loud is that people can only make informed choices if someone tells them the truth, even where the truth is uncomfortable[^the-sex-expert-casual-sex-is-almost-always-dange--pdCDq_MTj4].
