# Overview

In this episode, host Steven Bartlett interviews Dr Bill von Hippel, an evolutionary psychologist who has spent roughly four decades studying why humans do what they do, and author of "The Social Paradox: When Finding What You Want Means Losing What You Need". Von Hippel's central argument is that wealthy, educated, urban Westerners have badly misbalanced two evolved needs — autonomy and connection — and that our constant choice to "do our own thing" rather than connect is quietly making us less happy, less partnered, and less sexually active.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]

Von Hippel supports this with a run of statistics: the rise of living alone, collapsing neighborly contact, declining marriage and cohabitation in rich countries, and the finding that the Hadza hunter-gatherers — who bury nearly half their children and have no savings — report being happy at roughly twice the rate of Westerners. He argues that cities, wealth and education all amplify the autonomy problem because they let people stop needing each other, and he uses the same evolutionary lens to explain attraction (honest signals of quality such as risk-taking, humor and kindness in men; youth, health and body fat as fertility signals in women), the dating-app mismatch, the porn-for-sex substitution among young people, and collapsing fertility rates — which he attributes to the fact that humans evolved to want sex, not children.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]

The conversation also ranges into provocative territory — von Hippel argues a "perfect robot nanny" could be better than the imperfect human childcare we already outsource to — and closes with his practical prescriptions: prioritize family rituals like shared meals, sacrifice some autonomy to do things with friends, get a pet, be present in idle chit-chat, pursue lifestyle goals with a partner, and make connection habitual rather than a daily decision. He also shares unpublished findings from his work as a contracted scientist with WHOOP, including that exercise appears to amplify both good and bad behaviors' physiological effects.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]

# Key points

- Von Hippel argues the core modern mistake is overweighting autonomy over connection: in 1850 about 1 in 100 Americans lived alone versus 1 in 7 now, and neighborly contact has inverted since the 1970s — where one in three used to get together with neighbors regularly and one in five never did, the ratio has flipped.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- He cites research on the Hadza hunter-gatherers of Tanzania: over 90% reported being happy over the past week versus about 50% of Westerners, despite the Hadza burying almost half their children and having no savings — evidence, he says, that their autonomy–connection balance beats our material abundance.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- City dwellers are about 25% wealthier than rural people yet measurably less happy, he claims, because cities maximize autonomy and opportunity while eroding connection — urbanites are less likely to trust a neighbor with their house keys and are less satisfied with their friendships.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- On money, von Hippel invokes the Easterlin Paradox: individual income gains keep raising happiness well past $600,000 a year, yet the US is three times richer in real terms than in 1940 with happiness "exactly flat" — because money is largely a zero-sum status game.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- He describes attraction as driven by "honest signals of quality": for men, risk-taking (high-testosterone men pair up more, and testosterone drops after partnering), physical size, earned wealth, ambition, humor (a sign of an agile mind and good genes) and above all kindness, which he says "trumps everything" once minimal competence is met.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- For women, he argues male preferences track fertility signals — youth, health, and body fat (historically hard to attain and deposited on hips and thighs, where it supports fetal brain development) — noting the hip-to-waist ratio of Playboy models stayed constant from 1950 onward even as model weight swung dramatically; women, by contrast, evolved greater "sexual plasticity" and prioritize status and provisioning.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- Citing Brendan Zietsch's polygenic research, he claims straight men carrying more "gay genes" have more sexual partners than straight men with fewer — being "a little bit gay" is attractive to women, making homosexuality an evolutionary balancing act.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- He flags a growing education mismatch: women are about 60% of recent US college cohorts (three women for every two men on campus), while women tend to seek equally or more educated/resourced partners — on Tinder, roughly 20% of men get 80% of the swipes — leaving more people single and creating a societal risk from unpartnered, low-prospect men.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- Young Americans are having less sex than 20 years ago — the share of 18-to-25-year-olds who had sex in the past year fell from about 80% toward 65–70% while porn consumption roughly tripled — a substitution he dubs "SMILING" (social-media-induced laziness in our social habits); he also points to hormone-mimicking pollutants reducing sperm counts and sex drive.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- On fertility collapse, he argues humans "never evolved to want children" — only sex plus nurturance toward the babies that resulted — so once contraception decoupled the two, every industrialized country fell below the 2.1 replacement rate, with many countries on track to halve by 2100; citing Kahneman's experience-sampling work showing time with kids rates about as enjoyable as laundry, he says policy should strip the drudgery from parenting, even via robot nannies he'd trust over imperfect human ones.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- Using long-run German panel data, he reports marriage on average adds zero happiness — but couples who stay together are happiest in the marriage year itself (divorcing couples peaked the year before) — and argues that married people are about twice as likely to be "very happy" because less-individualistic people both marry and connect more; churchgoing shows a similar doubling of happiness, driven mostly by forced socializing, with the biggest gains among the rich, and regular attenders being roughly twice as likely to be happy.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- From his WHOOP performance-science work (unpublished, he cautions), he reports that exercise amplifies other behaviors — each alcoholic drink raises resting heart rate about 1.5 bpm and cuts HRV about 3 points, with worse effects on exercise days, while positive habits like dim lights or blue-light blockers help more on exercise days — and that men appear to sleep about 20 minutes less than women but recover more per hour of sleep.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
- His top five happiness prescriptions: prioritize family (especially shared meals and storytelling), sacrifice autonomy to do things with friends, get a pet, be present for idle conversation, and pursue lifestyle goals with a partner — plus spend money on experiences rather than possessions, since possessions are an unwinnable status game.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]

# Takeaways

Von Hippel's through-line is that evolution built humans to need both autonomy and connection, but modern wealth, cities and technology have made connection optional for the first time — and every convenient, autonomous choice we make (living alone, skipping the party, swiping instead of meeting, watching porn instead of pursuing sex) compounds into measurably lower happiness, fewer partnerships and fewer children. His prescription is not to abandon ambition — he explicitly endorses paying a loneliness price while young if you set a firm end date — but to re-engineer connection into habit: attach social contact to existing routines, do solitary pleasures with others, and stop treating each act of connection as a decision. He is candid that some of his claims (robot nannies, WHOOP findings, his belief that life is objectively meaningless but best spent being kind) are speculative or unvetted, and listeners should weigh them as one researcher's evolutionary framing rather than settled science.[^the-sex-psychologist-we-re-not-having-enough-sex-9gk3mNJs2FY]
