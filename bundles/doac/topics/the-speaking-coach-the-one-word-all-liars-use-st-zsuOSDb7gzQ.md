# Overview

In this episode Steven Bartlett interviews Jefferson Fisher, a fifth-generation board-certified trial attorney from Texas and author of *The Next Conversation: Argue Less, Talk More*, who has built an audience of roughly 12 million followers by teaching everyday communication tactics drawn from the courtroom.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ] Fisher's central argument is that communication ability is a disproportionate, "unfair advantage" in life — that jobs, relationships, and reputation come down almost entirely to what you decide to say next — and that the skills involved are learnable word-by-word rather than a matter of innate confidence or personality.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]

His framework has three pillars: say it with control (regulate fight-or-flight with breath and slowed speech), say it with confidence (confidence is the *outcome* of assertive behavior, not a prerequisite), and say it to connect (frame difficult conversations, get buy-in, and stay curious about the person behind the words).[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ] Running through all of it is his signature claim that you should never try to win an argument: winning the point typically loses the relationship, so arguments should be treated as knots to unravel — often with the single question "What am I missing?" — rather than contests to be won.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]

# Key points

- Fisher argues that trying to win an argument is self-defeating: "when you look to win an argument you will often lose the relationship," and having the last word usually just makes you first in line to apologize; instead he reframes arguments as knots to unravel together, using phrases like "help me find the knot" and "what am I missing".[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Rule one is "say it with control": he teaches clients to "let your breath be the first word," using a discreet version of the physiological sigh (two inhales through the nose, slow exhale) before responding, which he says keeps fight-or-flight from hijacking the analytical mind during disagreement.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Slowing your words down signals control: he demonstrates that "I already told you, I'm not going to do that" delivered slowly and without heat reads as near-total composure, whereas the fast, exasperated version signals that your buttons can be pushed.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Rule two is "say it with confidence," which he defines as an outcome rather than a starting state — "confidence is as assertive does" — meaning you acquire it only by doing assertive things, such as speaking directly ("I'm going to tell you this because I know you can handle it") rather than hedging.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- The word "just" is his number-one culprit for sounding weak and hesitant ("just wanted to check in"); removing it — "I wanted to check in with you" — creates forward, assertive momentum, and filler openers like "so, um, you can totally tell me if I'm wrong" are how low self-esteem shows up in sentence structure.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- He claims the more words it takes to tell the truth, the more it sounds like a lie; effective leaders and the wealthy write short, direct emails, while insecure people talk the most because "insecurities are very loud, confidence is very quiet".[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- On lying, he says silence is "the number one killer of liars," who want engagement so they can manage the narrative; extreme words — "never" and "always," as in "I never text when I drive" — are his dead-giveaway tell, along with instant, breathless answers, and simply repeating their words back slowly makes liars scramble to fix their story.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- His playbook for insults and disrespect: hold five to seven seconds of silence, ask them to say it again (people usually can't or won't repeat it — he recounts a mediation where one sister could not repeat "you're dead to me" on request), then use "questions of intent" like "did you mean for that to sound rude?" — which deny the provoker the dopamine hit of your visible hurt while forcing them to own their intent.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Over-apologizing corrodes self-worth "drip by drip"; he tells people-pleasers to swap apologies for gratitude — "thank you for waiting on me" instead of "sorry I'm late" — and to decline invitations by leading with the no ("I can't make it this go around"), followed by thanks and kindness, with no excuse or justification attached, since "but" erases everything said before it.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Rule three is "say it to connect," operationalized as a conversational frame for hard talks: state what you want to discuss, state how you want the conversation to end, and get the other person's buy-in ("that sound good?") — creating what he calls an invisible contract.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- "The person you see isn't the person you're talking to": he tells of a hostile deposition witness, Bobby, whose rage turned out to be about debt-collection letters over his mother's nursing home; triggers trace back to old wounds (Bartlett shares a friend whose "red mist" at being called wrong traced to childhood labels of being stupid), and minds change over hundreds of conversations, not one — illustrated with his two-glasses analogy that you cannot pour into a glass that is already full.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
- Practical guardrails he adds: never have 100% of a conversation on 10% of your battery; name your state out loud ("I can tell I'm getting defensive") because "when you claim it, you control it"; treat people you dislike as if they like you rather than handing them an enemy; and persuade through admitted imperfection — juries, like Lincoln's rivals-turned-cabinet, trust the side that concedes its flaws.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]

# Takeaways

The episode's throughline, as Fisher presents it, is that words are levers: tiny substitutions — dropping "just," trading apology for gratitude, breathing before speaking, asking "what am I missing" — compound into control, confidence, and connection, and what you choose to say next ripples outward to your career, your relationships, and even how your children will one day speak to theirs.[^the-speaking-coach-the-one-word-all-liars-use-st-zsuOSDb7gzQ]
