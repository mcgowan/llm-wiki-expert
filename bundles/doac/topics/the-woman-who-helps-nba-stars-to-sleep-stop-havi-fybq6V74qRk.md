# Overview

Sleep physician and performance researcher Dr Cheri Mah tells host Steven Bartlett that her working premise is simple: "if you don't sleep your best you will not be your best"[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk]. She describes a career spent translating sleep science into practice for professional sports organisations — the San Francisco Giants, the Golden State Warriors, the Philadelphia Eagles (where she says she earned a Super Bowl ring in 2017) — as well as for companies including Nike, Under Armour and ESPN, and for C-suite executives whose decisions she argues are just as sensitive to sleep loss as an athlete's reaction time[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].

Her central evidence is a sleep-extension study on a men's collegiate basketball team, in which players were encouraged to get nine to ten hours a night for five to seven weeks to pay back accumulated sleep debt. Mah reports 9% improvements in both free throws and three-point shots, 12% faster reaction time and a 4% improvement in sprint time, with sleep extended by roughly an hour and a half over baseline[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk]. She frames these as far larger than the 1% margins elite sport usually chases, and argues that sleep is a legal, safe, effective performance enhancer that most teams still leave on the table[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].

The rest of the conversation is practical. Mah walks through the sleep environment as a "cave" (dark, quiet, cool), the timing of showers and pre-sleep snacks, wind-down routines for a racing mind, nap length and the "nappuccino," snoozing and REM sleep, a pre-flight/in-flight/post-flight travel protocol, and the underdiagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk]. She is candid that as a new mother with an 11-month-old son she is not getting her own ideal sleep, and describes falling back on quality and timing when duration is out of reach[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].

# Key points

- Mah says the "badge of honour" belief that four or five hours is enough is finally shifting, helped by vocal athletes such as Tom Brady and Simone Biles; she notes the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend a minimum of seven hours, stresses this is a floor with wide individual variation — she says she personally feels terrible on seven — and argues improvement comes from adding 15 to 30 minutes a week, citing a high-school study in which 15 minutes more sleep was the difference between an A and a B student and 11 minutes the difference between a B and a C student[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- In her men's basketball sleep-extension study, published in 2011, players improved free throws by 9%, three-point shots by 9%, reaction time by 12% and sprint time by 4% — a baseline-to-half-court-to-baseline-to-full-court drill falling from about 16.2 to 15.5 seconds; on the downside risk she cites evidence that under six hours is associated with more fatigue-related injuries in adolescent athletes, that under eight hours carries roughly a 1.7-fold higher injury risk, and that her own biomechanics work found sleep-deprived athletes show far more variability in movements such as a vertical jump[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- Working with ESPN on the "NBA schedule alert project," Mah says she was 76-86% correct across three seasons at predicting when an NBA team was at highest risk of losing based purely on schedule and travel, deliberately excluding strength of team; she pairs this with a Monday Night Football analysis finding that betting on the West Coast team in East-versus-West night games would have beaten the Las Vegas point spread 68% of the time over 25 seasons, because a 7pm game sits closer to the West Coast body clock's 4-8pm peak performance window while feeling like 10pm to the East Coast team[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- Her flagship individual case is Golden State Warriors forward Andre Iguodala, who arrived in 2014 aged 28 having spent a decade staying up late gaming and taking two-to-three-hour naps; after months of work on wind-down routine, environment, nutrition and shortened naps timed nearer game time, his sleep rose from under seven to over eight hours and third-party analysis found a two-fold increase in three-point percentage, an 8.9% increase in free throws, a 29% improvement in points per minute and a 45% decrease in fouls — followed by a Finals MVP and, ultimately, four championships[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- Her "saved career" example is NFL center Ryan Jensen, cut by the Ravens to the practice squad, later diagnosed with sleep apnea and treated with CPAP; four years later he signed a $42 million contract and in 2021 won a Super Bowl with the Buccaneers — a condition Mah says affects roughly 26% of people aged 30-70 yet often goes undiagnosed for decades, which is why she warns that habitual snoring should not be treated as benign and notes that she treats young, fit professional athletes whose anatomy alone makes them susceptible[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- On the environment she recommends treating the bedroom as a dark, quiet, cool "cave": blackout curtains or an eye mask, earplugs or a white noise machine (including adaptive models that raise volume when a door slams), and a temperature of roughly 60-67°F (about 16-20°C), lowered a degree or two every few days to find a personal optimum[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- She advises moving hot showers and baths to an hour or two before bed rather than immediately before, because heat raises core temperature while falling asleep requires it to drop; done early, she says smaller studies suggest it can shorten time to sleep onset and enhance deep sleep — the stage carrying the largest growth-hormone pulse, concentrated in the first half of the night, with REM and its role in learning and memory consolidation weighted toward the early morning hours — which is also why she discourages repeated snoozing, arguing that setting the alarm for the actual wake time yields around 25 more minutes of consolidated REM than five snooze cycles of fragmented light sleep[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- For late-night hunger she endorses a pre-sleep snack of roughly 50% complex carbohydrate and 50% lean protein — cereal and milk, cottage cheese and fruit, wholewheat crackers and peanut butter, nuts with yoghurt and berries — while naming alcohol, caffeine, heavy fried and tomato-based foods as the combination that would most reliably wreck a night's sleep[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- For a racing mind she prescribes ten minutes of processing thoughts outside the bed in dim light — stretching, deep breathing to activate the parasympathetic system, journaling or a to-do list — comparing it to easing off the brakes rather than slamming them on at 60mph, insists bed be reserved for sleep and sex only so the brain stops associating it with wakefulness, and recommends naps of no more than 30 minutes to stay in lighter stages and avoid sleep inertia, including the "nappuccino" of caffeine taken immediately before a 20-30 minute nap, which she says beats either alone for alertness[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- On sleep debt she says it accumulates and can be partially repaid — a week or two of extension delivering the biggest return, with one preliminary study in professional baseball players showing five days of one extra hour improved reaction time and processing speed — but denies you can bank surplus sleep in advance, and notes that three nights of recovery sleep did not restore reaction time to baseline for those who had been on seven, five or three hours[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- Her travel protocol covers all three phases: don't panic-pack, bank sleep and pre-shift the body clock by half an hour a day with morning sunlight before departure; hydrate, minimise caffeine and adopt destination meal and sleep timing in flight; and on arrival allow roughly a day per time zone crossed, use sunglasses to avoid light at the wrong hours, avoid critical meetings or maximal exercise on day one, and deploy caffeine and short naps through the 1-4pm dip[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
- She endorses working with rather than against chronotype where possible — Bartlett schedules nothing before 11am — and points to California's newly enacted later high-school and middle-school start times, backed by evidence of higher attendance, better GPA, fewer mental health problems and fewer car accidents, as a structural fix for adolescents whose body clocks naturally run late and who otherwise accumulate debt by being forced up early[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].

# Takeaways

Mah's argument is that sleep is not a residual left over at the end of the day but a controllable input with quantifiable output, and that the gains available — 9% on free throws, 12% on reaction time — dwarf the marginal improvements elite performers usually chase[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk]. She rejects the "I don't have time to sleep" objection, arguing that everyone has five minutes for a wind-down routine and can optimise their environment, and warns against the athlete's instinct to defer sleep work to the off-season, since arriving at the season already in debt means playing catch-up against opponents who are not[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk]. Her closing reframe is the line she gives her athletes: sleep is not the end of today but the beginning of tomorrow[^the-woman-who-helps-nba-stars-to-sleep-stop-havi-fybq6V74qRk].
