# Overview

The episode is built in two parts: a short call Steven Bartlett records with Tom Aspinall hours after Jon Jones's retirement makes him the UFC's undisputed heavyweight champion, followed by a longer studio interview taped weeks earlier while the Jones fight was still hanging over him.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY] Aspinall is careful to say he was never chasing Jones personally — "ultimately the thing that I wanted really was the heavyweight title" — and that having gone nearly a year without a fight while healthy and training was the frustrating part.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]

The interview's central theme is fear. Aspinall says flatly that he is scared to fight everybody and that any fighter who claims otherwise is "either lying to yourself or you're a complete idiot," given that a walkout ends with you potentially "separated from your own consciousness" in front of millions.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY] Rather than suppressing it, he describes accepting it — "I bathe in the fear" — and treating it as the only fuel that works, illustrating the point with a friend who lifted a wardrobe off his child and then could not shift it minutes later.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY] He argues training is roughly 80% physical and 20% mental, but fight night flips to 80% mental, and that the time fighters devote to each is wildly out of proportion.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]

The second half is about the unglamorous parts: the years with no money and three children under 25, the knee injury that ended a title eliminator in 15 seconds in front of 25,000 home fans, and his son's autism diagnosis — a subject he raises to argue that British families without the money to pay privately are stuck on multi-year waiting lists while their children regress.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]

# Key points

- Aspinall says he decided at nine years old, on seeing adults at his father's gym who had no regular job, that he would be UFC heavyweight champion; he grew up in gyms in Atherton, Greater Manchester, training alongside 40-year-olds because there were no children's classes.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- His argument for martial arts as a purpose-builder for young men: it checks the ego weekly because "every time you step onto a mat you're getting hit in the face with reality," and the consequences of inconsistency arrive immediately rather than in a decade — unlike sitting on a sofa at 18, where the cost is invisible.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- On the economics of the sport, he says he earned £50–100 per fight across nine amateur bouts and £200 for his first professional fight; that UFC newcomers typically start on around $10,000–15,000 show money with a matching win bonus over a four-fight contract that can be terminated at any time; and that he would be surprised if one fighter out of the 40 on a decent regional card ever bought a house from MMA. He estimates 95% of people he has known in gyms have never made more than £5,000 for a fight.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- His first six-figure payday came at the end of 2023 against Sergei Pavlovich, a title fight he took on two weeks' notice — roughly 23 years after he started training. He describes his career graph as flat for a very long time then suddenly steep: "it takes years to become an overnight success."[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- The object he brings in the black box is a knee pad. He fought and trained "with one leg" for years, never kicking or kneeling with it, until the knee gave out 15 seconds into a title eliminator against Curtis Blaydes at the O2 Arena in London — the most devastating moment of his career, followed by surgery and six months on the sofa.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- He credits that injury with the most growth of anything in his life: superstition had stopped him changing anything while he was winning, and the enforced pause let him cut out toxic people and pointless training sessions. He also says he came close to retiring then, and that online commenters writing him off — suggesting punditry instead — is what convinced him to come back: "I can't let regular people tell me how to function."[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- On his mental training he describes "mentally drilling" the walkout thousands of times so the real thing feels normal, writing simple affirmations by hand and keeping them in his bedside table, and working with a hypnotherapist he is increasing to twice weekly — which he says lowers his anxiety and helps him sleep, while stressing it is his personal experience and not expertise.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- He describes long-standing anxiety and OCD — as a child he could not settle unless curtains, drawers and the television angle were exactly right — and says it flares when life is stressful, most recently when one of his sons was hospitalised. For fights he now deletes social media entirely and plans a separate "training camp phone" for his wife, parents and camp only.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- His assessment of Jon Jones: an immortal of the sport and one of the smartest fighters ever, whose edge is distance management and forcing elite opponents to fight his style, and who has "chosen very very wisely" — a point Aspinall initially declines to phrase as avoiding dangerous opponents before conceding, "yeah, I probably am saying that."[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- He treats his own lack of footage as a weapon: he holds the record for the fastest average fight-ending time, so a film-study fighter like Jones has barely three or four minutes of tape on him, and he says he has never gone out trying to finish quickly — "that's the problem of my opponents."[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- His recovery rule of thumb is roughly half as much recovery as training — two hours for a four-hour training day — via stretching, sauna, swimming, breathing work and napping, plus keeping a written food-and-feeling log rather than following any prescribed diet.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- The calm he shows in the octagon is deliberate, learned from Georges St-Pierre: he walks around all fight day fake-smiling with his shoulders back and head up, on the theory that "sooner or later my mind will actually start to follow my body's lead," while inside he is "scared to death" and looking at his opponent "like I'm in the queue for a sandwich."[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- On his son's autism, he recounts a year on an NHS waiting list, denial that lockdown was the cause, watching Paddy McGuinness's documentary, then paying privately for a diagnosis obtained through a contact — and being explicit that his financial position is what made that possible. Without a diagnosis, he says, parents are "treading water" with no funding, no one-to-one support and no direction; he calls the multi-year waits a serious crisis while praising the NHS's emergency care.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
- Asked about life after fighting, he admits concern about what replaces a routine that consumes his whole day, says he would rather retire "a little bit too early than a little bit too late" having seen how fighters end up, and notes that titles and money did not change what goes on inside his head — the problems simply changed.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]

# Takeaways

Aspinall presents his rise as a case against the idea of talent revealing itself quickly: two decades of gym time, nine amateur fights for pocket money, borrowed cash for nappies and fuel, and a career-threatening injury before anything visible happened.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY] What he says carried him through was outlasting people, a father and wife who talked him out of quitting, and a relationship with fear that he treats as an asset rather than something to be suppressed.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY] He closes by rejecting the framing that he was born special — "I wasn't born special… I believe that anybody can be special" — while insisting that children from working-class towns are told to aim lower than they should.[^tom-aspinall-opens-up-about-brain-damage-his-fut-puc5pZVsFPY]
