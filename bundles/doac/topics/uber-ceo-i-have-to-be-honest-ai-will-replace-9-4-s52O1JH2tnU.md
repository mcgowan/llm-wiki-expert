# Overview

Steven Bartlett interviews Dara Khosrowshahi, CEO of Uber and former CEO of Expedia, whose story begins with his family fleeing Iran after the 1978 revolution — revolutionary guards' bullets came through their living room — and rebuilding from nothing in New York while his father was trapped in Iran for six years.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU] Khosrowshahi says the experience left him with a core feeling of never being safe, a drive to make his family proud, and a relentlessness he considers the defining trait of successful technology companies; he becomes visibly emotional discussing his late father, whose loss of livelihood and "value to the world" he says destroyed him.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]

The conversation covers his path from investment banking at Allen & Company, through M&A under Barry Diller (buying Ticketmaster, Match.com, Expedia, and Hotels.com during the shift of commerce online), to running Expedia for 12 years and then taking over a crisis-ridden Uber in 2017, turning annual losses of $2.5-3 billion into what he says is now $9.8 billion in yearly free cash flow.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU] He shares his management philosophy: radical transparency as the price of getting truth back from your team, betting on people, "losing, learning, next," exponential rather than linear thinking, and hard work as a learned skill.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]

The final act confronts AI and autonomous vehicles. Khosrowshahi is unusually direct for a sitting CEO: he estimates AI will be capable of the work 70-80% of humans do within roughly 10 years for intellectual jobs (15-20 for physical ones), concedes he does not know what Uber's 9.5 million drivers and couriers will do as autonomy matures, and argues society's missing capability is a "retraining machine."[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]

# Key points

- Khosrowshahi says Uber has 9.5 million drivers and couriers, calling it the world's largest organizer of flexible work — he quips the second largest workforce is the Chinese army — and admits that in 15-20 years the majority of trips could be fulfilled by robots, answering "I don't know" when asked what those 9 million people will do.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He predicts AI will be able to replace the work 70-80% of humans can do within 10 years for intellectual jobs and 15-20 years for physical jobs, and argues 10 years is not enough time for society to adjust — unlike the generations-long farming transition.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- His view is that you cannot slow the pace of change ("China won't"), so leaders should lean in and shape how the change lands; he names Anthropic's Dario Amodei as a technology leader having the right public discussions.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- On the upside of autonomy, he notes roughly a million people die in road accidents worldwide each year (35,000-40,000 in the US) and argues safer, cheaper autonomous transport — via Jevons paradox — expands access and returns human lives.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He reports about 90% of Uber's coders use AI tools, with a 30% cohort of power users showing productivity gains he has never seen before; near-term he says he would hire more engineers as they get more efficient, but concedes that in about five years he may add agents and GPUs instead of headcount.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- His stated threshold for "we are all replaceable" is real-time learning: today's models are pre-trained and frozen rather than learning continuously like humans, and until that changes he sees human orchestration jobs surviving.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He argues UBI is not the answer, claiming basic-income tests have shown recipients doing worse on outcomes, and — echoing his father's story — that jobs provide worth and meaning, not just money.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- His leadership doctrine is transparency as self-defense: a CEO who bullshits his staff teaches them to filter the truth back, so he tells hard truths even if it scares people away — "then they can leave."[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He maintains random direct channels — regularly meeting engineers four levels down — because CEO failures usually come from wrong data, not wrong decisions, and staff naturally filter what reaches the top.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He calls working hard "the most important skill in life," a learned skill visible in elite athletes like Ronaldo and Michael Jordan, says Uber's values include "embrace the grind," "go get it," and his own hand-written "do the right thing, period," and neither he nor Bartlett can name someone who wasn't a hard worker becoming one.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- Key career lessons he recounts include Barry Diller's "they won, we lost, next" response to losing the Paramount bid, taking direct operational control of Expedia.com after two failed hires, and overpaying — relative to market opinion, not eventual reality — for every great company he bought, because people project linearly while winning technologies compound exponentially.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
- He credits Spotify founder Daniel Ek's challenge — "since when is life about being happy? It's about making impact" — and his father's advice that "when a company who's a verb tells you to run it, you just say yes" for his 2017 jump from Expedia to a then-chaotic Uber; his advice to young people is to work hard and avoid rigid career plans: "before you go out and try to change the world, let the world change you first."[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]

# Takeaways

The episode's through-line, as the host frames it, is honesty in transition: a CEO who answers the question rather than pivoting, applied to the most uncomfortable question in his own business — that the technology making Uber safer and cheaper will eventually displace the workforce that built it.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU] Khosrowshahi's position is that displacement is coming faster than society can currently absorb, that neither UBI nor wishful "it'll be fine" talk is an answer, and that the one investment he would make is a national-scale retraining capability — while individuals, in his view, should bet on hard work, openness, and curiosity over rigid plans.[^uber-ceo-i-have-to-be-honest-ai-will-replace-9-4-s52O1JH2tnU]
