# Overview

In this episode of The Diary Of A CEO, host Steven Bartlett — newly engaged — asks New York divorce lawyer James Sexton, author of *How to Stay in Love* and a veteran of 25 years "facilitating the demise of marriages," how not to end up as one of his clients. Sexton's central diagnosis is that marriages rarely die from a single catastrophic event; they die from "slippage" — a gradually accumulating series of small disconnections that individually seem too trivial to raise, until the presenting reason for divorce (an affair, a blow-up fight, a financial failure) arrives on top of a relationship that had already "lost the plot."[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]

Sexton argues that culture sets couples up to fail by portraying love as effortless — he calls the romcom "an emotional version of pornography" — so any discomfort gets read as evidence of being with the wrong person, and people avoid the small uncomfortable conversations that would prevent drift. He contends the deeper obstacle is fear: most people's fundamental terror is that they are not worthy of love, that "if you knew me, you wouldn't love me," which makes them afraid to ask a partner what they love about them or what they are getting wrong.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]

The conversation ranges from Sexton's signature prescriptions — a weekly ritual of naming three things you love about your partner, treating relationship maintenance like a job with performance reviews, offering a "menu" of support options when a partner is struggling — to a hands-on case for prenups (illustrated with M&Ms as "yours, mine, and ours" buckets), petnups, gray divorce trends, and Sexton's own story: an alcoholic father, a fear of asking for help, and a dream about his late mother that changed how he relates to the people he loves.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]

# Key points

- Sexton's number one predicted reason a spouse of a high achiever files for divorce is not betrayal but attention: she "feels herself slipping in the rankings" of priorities — a partner who is a great provider and protector but has stopped seeing and noticing her. He says this pattern alone "will keep me in business for the rest of my life."[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- He distinguishes the "presenting reason" for divorce (affair, terrible fight, financial failure) from the underlying reason — "we lost the plot" — and defines "slippage" as small disconnections that individually mean nothing but accumulate: "no single raindrop is responsible for the flood."[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- Sexton argues people spot slippage in the moment but avoid raising it because aversion to pain beats desire for joy "every single time" — the same asymmetry, he notes, that made the opiate crisis bigger than a cocaine crisis — and the cultural narrative that love should be easy frames any discomfort as a sign you're doing it wrong.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- His signature ritual: once a week, tell your partner three things you love about them; the "advanced version" adds three things they did that week that made you feel loved, three things they could have done better, and — to end on fun — three things that made you desire them. He counters the "my partner would cringe" objection: write it down instead, and ask what is really uncomfortable about the exercise.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- Echoing the Dalai Lama meditation story, he says if you don't have five minutes a week to devote to your relationship, "then you're going to need hours" — the subject of his next book on a systematic approach to being good at love.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- On infidelity, Sexton says men get *caught* cheating more than women, not that they necessarily cheat more; in his experience women's affairs usually signal the relationship is already over, while men often tell him the affair "had nothing to do with my wife" — behavior he compares to eating potato chips left in the cabinet when tired and depleted.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- He reframes dating as a job interview and marriage as the job, arguing couples should normalize "performance reviews" and non-defensive check-ins — framing observations as "something has changed, have you noticed?" rather than accusations — and borrowing his courtroom tactic of apologizing first to defuse aggression.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- When a partner is struggling, he recommends offering a "menu" — just listen, give solutions, distract with a funny story, physical affection, a walk — because partners' needs differ and mind-reading fails; he notes he only understands himself "about 70%" after 20 years of therapy, so expecting a spouse to intuit 100% is unfair.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- Sexton strongly advocates prenups: the rules of your divorce "will either be written by the state legislature or by you and she," and he trusts a chosen partner over the government. Using M&Ms, he illustrates separate versus marital ("we") property, warning that without a prenup co-mingled assets become community property — in California at the seven-year mark — and that he has seen clients time filings around seven- and twenty-year thresholds that change asset division and alimony formulas.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- If you're scared to raise a prenup with your partner, he says, that is precisely why you should — marriage requires becoming accustomed to hard conversations; he also created a free "petnup" site (proceeds to animal shelters) after courts treated pets as chattel, citing a New York judge who ordered a disputed dog sold and the proceeds divided.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- On statistics, Sexton cites a 56% divorce rate while cautioning that official figures only count registered dissolutions, missing couples who separate without finalizing; the discussion notes "gray divorce" (over-50) has doubled since 1990 and tripled for over-65s — driven, they suggest, by longer, healthier lives, women's financial independence, and reduced stigma — while millennial and Gen Z divorce rates have fallen as people marry later and more selectively.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
- Sexton warns against two contradictory assumptions at the altar: that marriage will change your partner, and that nothing will change — both, he says, lead directly to his desk; the only reconciliation is to keep talking about change without judgment as it happens.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]

# Takeaways

Sexton's closing register is unexpectedly tender for a divorce lawyer: every marriage ends "in death or divorce," and the best possible ending is a partner who can say "this person helped me become the most authentic version of myself, and they're still my favorite person." He argues love is an act of courage precisely because it means giving someone the opportunity to hurt you, that accomplishment is a poor substitute for feeling worthy of love, and — drawing on a dream about his late mother — that sometimes what matters most is not the words but simply being next to the people you love.[^world-no-1-divorce-lawyer-if-you-do-this-your-ma-Uvy5mcLiWW0]
