# Overview

Alex Honnold — the free-solo climber famous for ropeless ascents of El Capitan (the subject of the film *Free Solo*) and, most recently, a live-televised climb of the Taipei 101 skyscraper — talks with Steven Bartlett about fear, risk, mastery, and what actually produced his career. Honnold pushes back hard on the "he's just built different" narrative, particularly the famous amygdala brain scan from *Free Solo*: he argues the scan simply shows that after 20 years of climbing five days a week and "being really freaking scared," black-and-white photos in a safe fMRI tube no longer trigger him — conditioning, not a neurological anomaly. His real takeaway, he says, is that "I have an amygdala and it works," and that humans have tremendous capacity to retrain fear through exposure, citing his own transformation from terrified public speaker to comfortable one.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]

The episode traces his unglamorous origin: an unemotional household, parents in a fraught marriage who stayed together for the kids, a perfectionist mother, and a father who died suddenly at 55 — a mortality reminder Honnold credits with shaping his philosophy that since you die either way, you should take smart, calculated, *chosen* risks rather than the unexamined risks of drunk driving or sedentary living. He describes living in a van on a few hundred dollars a month from roughly age 20 to 30, earning around $10k a year from his first North Face sponsorship, and doing enormous amounts of free work before *Free Solo* and the Taipei climb sent his earnings vertical in a "winner take all" economy.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]

Interwoven is an unusually personal thread: Bartlett reads aloud a letter from Honnold's wife Sanni about his low emotionality and his "hawk-like" attention as a form of love, and Honnold discusses acts of service versus verbal affection, his two daughters, and the Honnold Foundation, which funds community solar projects worldwide.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]

# Key points

- Honnold rejects the "fearless mutant" reading of his *Free Solo* brain scan: the fMRI showed his amygdala lighting up less to scary black-and-white photos, but he argues that being physically safe in a metal tube looking at pictures simply can't scare someone who has been genuinely scared for 20 years — like a monk, his brain responds differently because of conditioning, not structure.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- His stated "hack" for fear is that there is no hack: "you just get really freaking scared over and over for so long and eventually it's not that scary anymore" — and unlike "gravity-assisted sports" where you can commit in one 3-2-1 moment, climbing is slow, so fear creeps in move by move and must be managed continuously.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- The overnight-success illusion: viewers saw the 2017 El Capitan free solo as a two-year project from the film, but Honnold had climbed El Cap roughly 60 times over about a decade of stepwise progression, and has climbed five days a week for 30 years — "after 30 years of practice, I just walked up and did it."[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- His method for impossible-looking goals is decomposition: for Taipei 101 he scouted every distinct segment (slab base, overhanging "dragon" corners, balconies) with ropes in September, kept phone notes on each section like studying any subject, then executed the roughly two-hour free climb in January — even using a bolted security camera as a hold at the hardest move.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- He says Taipei 101 was "obviously much easier" than El Capitan — which was a secret 10-year life project done on his own terms — but sat in his "sweet spot" of hard-but-doable, since the absolute limit of ability cannot be attempted on live TV; Netflix ran a 10-second delay, and he says more than half of Netflix subscribers in Taiwan watched.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- On mortality and risk, Honnold argues people behave as if they'll live forever: his father died unexpectedly at 55, and he contends it's better to die at 55 having done things you're proud of than at 78 wishing you'd lived more — everyone is taking risks (drunk driving, heart disease from sedentary living); the difference is he chooses his intentionally and mitigates them.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- He claims free soloing is statistically safer than perceived — comparable, he suspects, to recreational skiing — and notes most elite free soloists who died were killed in adjacent activities like wingsuit BASE jumping or a rogue wave, not while soloing; climbing is "binary: either you're totally safe or you're going to die, and the odds of dying are very small."[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- His scariest career moments came with ropes on, not soloing: on a 2017 Antarctica expedition the crumbling rock offered no protection placements (risking ~400-foot falls on a 200-foot rope), and he alternated climbing days with "shell-shocked" tent days spooning Nutella — because when soloing he keeps a healthy margin, but a rope tempts you to push into the unknown.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- Honnold deliberately visualizes falling — assessing whether a fall means hitting a ledge or swinging safely into free space — arguing you must confront negative outcomes rationally to distinguish real danger from a mind "running away from you," then breathe and carry on.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- On money and career, he advises "don't get hung up on how much you get paid, just do the thing, make sure it's freaking rad and it all sorts itself out" — he did years of free films that compounded into a National Geographic cover, a 2011 60 Minutes profile, and eventually *Free Solo*; he found the reported fee for Taipei "embarrassingly high" by climbing standards yet small next to boxing purses.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- Against grand life plans, he favors many appropriately-sized goals — "Taipei 4 before Taipei 101" — holding "strong opinions loosely held," noting you can't predict which achievements will matter (some speed records fall next season, others stand 15 years), echoing Bartlett's Steve Jobs point that dots only connect looking backwards.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- Asked for one last climb, Honnold names free soloing the Yosemite "Triple" — El Capitan, Half Dome, and Mount Watkins in a day, which he has soloed with ropes in 18-19 hours but no one has ever free soloed — calling it the obvious "next generation" challenge; he also revealed he has twice scouted the Burj Khalifa but found it too extreme, with 112 repetitions of a near-maximal span move.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
- Through the Honnold Foundation he has given roughly a third of his income since 2012 to cover overheads, with over $13 million granted across ~130 community solar projects in 30 countries, impacting about 650,000 people, creating 1,200+ jobs, and helping protect 15 million acres of biodiverse forest.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]

# Takeaways

Honnold's closing message is deliberately deflating to his own myth: he describes himself as a middle-class suburban kid from an unathletic family whose only real edge is loving climbing enough to put in 30 years of effort — and he insists the same conditioning is available to anyone willing to take embarrassingly small first steps toward the thing they love. Since the ending is fixed — "you're going to freaking die either way" — his prescription is to fill the space before it with intentional, well-prepared risks and things you're proud of, and, where possible, pair the inspiration with direct material impact like his solar-access foundation.[^world-s-greatest-climber-if-i-had-one-last-climb-ajgwabD4_HE]
